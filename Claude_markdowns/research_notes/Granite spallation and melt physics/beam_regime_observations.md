# Beam-heated granite and basalt: observed regimes (MMW, microwave, laser)

Scope: what has been OBSERVED (and, separately, MODELLED) when granite and other
crystalline rocks are heated by millimetre-wave, microwave or laser beams, with the
intensity, spot size, exposure and outcome, as the first points of a regime map
(power density × exposure × absorption depth × spot size → spall / crack / melt / vaporise).

Unit conventions used throughout: 1 W/cm² = 1×10⁴ W/m²; 1 kW/cm² = 1×10⁷ W/m².
"Derived" marks a number I computed from the source's stated power and aperture; the
source did not print it. "Abstract/snippet only" marks sources whose full text I could
not open (listed again under Gaps).

Local sources used (on disk):
- `tests/MMWSpalling/validation/zhang/zhang_oglesby_mmw_validation_summary.txt` (digitised Oglesby 2014 / Zhang 2023 Fig. 7)
- `franck_material/Walsh_2013_ModelingThermallyInducedFailure_LLNL-TR-632239.pdf` and the three Walsh/Lomov GRC/SGW papers
- Downloaded to scratchpad and text-extracted: PSFC/JA-17-8 (Woskov 2017), PSFC/RR-16-5 (Woskov & Oglesby 2016), Hartlieb & Grafe BHM 2017, Jerby 2004 poster, DOE 2013 peer-review slides, Quaise GRC 2021, arXiv 2011.14624 review.

Absorption-depth anchors for the fourth axis: Zhang 2023 uses 14 Np/m (solid) and 56 Np/m (liquid) granite at the Oglesby 28 GHz condition (1/e depths ≈ 71 mm and 18 mm; model inputs). Shteinman, Anker & Einat 2024 (W-band, 75–110 GHz, eight Israeli hard-rock samples) measured "90 to 99 % per centimeter" power absorption, i.e. α ≈ 230–460 Np/m, 1/e depth ≈ 2–4 mm (derived), "relatively independent of frequency" but higher at the upper W band. Jerby's basalt becomes electrically conducting (~0.02 Ω m) near 1100 °C, so the 2.45 GHz skin depth collapses on melting — the thermal-runaway root.

## Q9: Has anyone reported spallation (flaking) under MMW, microwave or laser heating of granite or similar rock, with intensity, spot, frequency, exposure and outcome?

### Takeaway
Spallation as the primary removal regime is documented quantitatively only for lasers (1–5×10⁷ W/m², sub-second dwell, Nd:YAG/CO₂, mostly sedimentary rocks with granite in the wider programme) and, as surface "spalling/bursting", for 2.45 GHz microwaves on basalt at 6 kW. In the entire MIT/Quaise millimetre-wave record (28–45 GHz, ~10⁶–2×10⁷ W/m², 2–5 cm spots, 30 s–40 min) the observed outcome is melting, then vaporisation; thermal fracturing of the sample and granular wall disintegration are reported as side effects, never flaking as a removal mechanism. No MMW spallation threshold exists in the literature I could reach.

### Cited Findings
- MMW melt onset: Oglesby's granite slab reached 1410 K at 50 s and 1716 K at 150 s under 800 W on a 2 cm waist (peak ≈1.3×10⁶ W/m², derived), then 2400–2870 K at 2300–3200 W, never reaching the 3233–3503 K vaporisation band — [Zhang et al. 2023, Computers & Geotechnics 161](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0266352X23003282) ([arXiv 2211.00681](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.00681)); digitised table in the local file `tests/MMWSpalling/validation/zhang/zhang_oglesby_mmw_validation_summary.txt`.
- "Both granite and basalt would melt within 2–3 minutes of beam exposure at about 2 kW incident power. Increasing incident power to 4.5 kW would typically raise the melt temperature into the 2500–3000 °C range in 5–10 minutes." Launcher 20 mm TE11, ~50° divergence, 50 mm hole at 35 mm standoff — [Woskov 2017, PSFC/JA-17-8](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/17ja/17ja008/17ja008_full.pdf).
- "The very high temperature gradient (> 400 °C/cm) between the heated spot and the rest of the rock caused significant thermal fracturing … All the laboratory samples had to be constrained by steel bands around the periphery to keep them from breaking apart" — [Woskov 2017](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/17ja/17ja008/17ja008_full.pdf). This is bulk thermal cracking, not surface flaking.
- "Experiments at 28 GHz show that with peak intensities of 1–2 kW/cm² granite, basalt, and limestone can be melted and vaporized to over 3000 °C in a few minutes"; 2.5–4.0 kW TE11 from a 2 cm aperture made a "granite black melt crater" — [Woskov & Einstein, Penetrating rock with intense millimeter-waves (PSFC/JA-14-17)](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/14ja/14ja017/abstract.php) (snippets only).
- At 45 GHz, 5 kW CW for 30 s gave 552 W/cm² (5.5×10⁶ W/m²) and a peak of 968.9 °C (no melt); 15 kW for 60 s gave 1650 W/cm² (1.65×10⁷ W/m²) and 2032.6 °C (melt) — [Liu et al. 2022, J. Infrared Millim. THz Waves](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10762-022-00855-5). A search snippet attributed a "spallation-type" ridge-ablation remark to a gyrotron paper; I could not locate it in any text I opened and treat it as unverified.
- Granite borehole wall under 28 GHz melt heating: "The diameter of the pre drilled granite hole was increased … to about 2 9/16" by 2 3/8" … The material coming off the wall formed a 5/16" (0.8 cm) maximum of loose sand-like rubble" — the closest MMW observation to spall-like disintegration, produced at the melt rim, not as a drilling mechanism — [Woskov & Oglesby 2016, PSFC/RR-16-5](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/16rr/16rr005/16rr005_full.pdf).
- MIT's own patent assigns MMW to melting/vaporisation ("Axial material will be volatilized creating a pressure to force the remaining melt material forward and into the sides making dense glassy walls") and mentions spallation only as "short high peak power pulses, not long enough to cause melting, to fracture the glassy/ceramic wall" — [US 8,393,410 B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8393410B2/en).
- Quaise/MIT/ORNL name the target regime "full bore vaporization" and contrast it with their low-power "melt drilling" tests (1–2 kW absorbed, 1:1 holes in basalt with a leak hole); no spallation regime is described — [Houde et al. 2021, GRC Trans. 45](https://publications.mygeoenergynow.org/grc/1034509.pdf).
- Laser spallation window: "short illumination by a single laser beam of irradiance about 1000 to 5000 W/cm²" (1–5×10⁷ W/m²) spalls; the programme swept 10⁶ → 10³ W/cm² through "vaporized, melted, and spalled"; shale at 4217 W/cm²: 0.5 s → spalling at 0.52 kJ/cm³, 1.0 s → melting at 3.6 kJ/cm³; Berea sandstone CO₂ 712 W/cm², 0.5 s → spalling at 2.6 kJ/cm³ — [US 7,416,258 B2 (Gahan, Parker, Batarseh et al.)](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7416258B2/en); thresholds ≈920 W/cm² (Berea) and 784 W/cm² (shale) — [SPE/GTI summaries via search](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/254511679_Laser_Drilling_Determination_of_Energy_Required_to_Remove_Rock) (snippet).
- "the lowest SE values are obtained in the spalling zone just prior to the onset of mineral melt"; "maximum specific power ought be used … provided that spallation precedes melting" — [Xu, Reed, Graves 2004, Laser spallation of rocks for oil well drilling](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/328361783_Laser_spallation_of_rocks_for_oil_well_drilling) (abstract only).
- Granite laser boundaries quoted as 230 W/cm² (2.3×10⁶ W/m²) for spallation onset and an "estimated" 5200 W/cm² (5.2×10⁷ W/m²) for the spallation→melting transition — [Research Square rs-7725822](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-7725822/v1) (abstract only; one snippet gives ~300 W/cm²).
- Pulsed Nd:YAG perforation of granite needs 30.3 kW/cm² (3.0×10⁸ W/m²) to create a cavity (ablation, not spallation) — [Ahmadi et al. 2011, Opt. Laser Technol.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0030399210001635) (abstract).
- 2.45 GHz, open-type applicator: granite at 6 kW/360 s → 670.5 °C with "cracking and melting"; specimens "weakened by cracking at 3–5 kW and by melting and spalling at 6 kW due to thermal runaway"; basalt showed "surface spalling, bursting or melting" — [Lu et al. 2019, Rock Mech. Rock Eng. 52:3017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00603-019-1743-y) and the [Int. J. Rock Mech. Min. Sci. 2019 paper](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1365160919302771) (abstract/snippets).
- 2.45 GHz, 24 kW open-ended waveguide, 30 s per spot on a 50×50×30 cm granite block: surface ≈150 °C, extensive crack network, no melt or spall; cutting force −10 % — [Hartlieb & Grafe 2017, BHM 162:77](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00501-016-0569-0.pdf).
- 3.2 kW multimode cavity: basalt 100/280/450 °C at 30/60/120 s, microcracks after 40 s, wide cracks after 120 s; 2 kW/120 s cracked and melted gabbro/monzonite/granite; 1.4 kW granite cubes "melted and cracked at 600 °C and completely melted at 800 °C" — [arXiv 2011.14624 review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.14624) (secondary).
- Flame-jet anchor (modelled): Weibull ROP 0–25 m/h over 0–1×10⁷ W/m² at surface temperatures 300–600 K; variable spall aspect ratio raises the flux for a given ROP by ~15 % — Walsh 2013, LLNL-TR-632239, Fig. 14 (local `franck_material/`). The LLNL reports mention laser/microwave only as alternative heat sources in their introductions; they contain no MMW regime statement (grep of all four PDFs).

### Inferences
- The MMW observations sit at 10⁶–2×10⁷ W/m² with 2–5 cm spots and exposures of 30 s to tens of minutes; every one melted. The laser spallation window (1–5×10⁷ W/m²) overlaps that intensity range but uses sub-second dwell and a surface-absorbing wavelength. The discriminator in the data is therefore exposure time and absorption depth, not intensity alone: at MMW absorption depths (1/e ≈ 2–7 cm at 28 GHz per Zhang's α, 2–4 mm in W-band) the near-surface gradient that drives flaking is weaker and the surface reaches T_m before a spall-depth layer is stressed.
- The user's 10 GHz observation (spall at lower intensity, melt at higher) has no published counterpart; the nearest brackets are the laser 2.3×10⁶ (onset) to 5.2×10⁷ W/m² (melt transition) figures for granite, whose provenance is weak.
- Liu's 45 GHz point (5.5×10⁶ W/m² for 30 s → 969 °C, no melt) and Oglesby's (1.3×10⁶ W/m² for 150 s → 1716 K, melt) together bound the melt onset at MMW frequencies to roughly 10⁶ W/m² × 10² s or 10⁷ W/m² × 10¹ s, a fluence of order 10⁸ J/m².

### Gaps
- No MMW spallation threshold or flake observation exists in any source I could open; the "spallation-type" snippet could not be traced.
- Oglesby et al. 2014 final report ([OSTI 1169951](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1169951)) could not be fetched (connection reset twice); numbers are taken from Zhang 2023 and the local digitised table. An "Oglesby PhD thesis" was not found; Ken Oglesby (Impact Technologies) appears as a co-author, not a thesis author.
- Woskov & Einstein 2014 full text (ResearchGate 403), Woskov & Michael 2011 full text (DSpace 429 rate limit, Springer paywall), Lu et al. 2019 full texts (ScienceDirect/Springer 403), Ma et al. 2022 Wiley (403), the DOE laser Phase-1 report ([OSTI 924031](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/924031-pyPnBr/), socket closed), Gahan et al. SPE 71466, Graves & O'Brien 1998, Xu et al. 2004 full text, Nekoovaght/Hassani, Jamali et al. 2019 — all unread beyond abstracts/snippets, so laser granite thresholds carry only abstract-level provenance.
- Spot sizes for the 2.45 GHz open-ended-waveguide tests are not printed; power density there is order-of-magnitude only.

### Verdict
No one has reported spallation of granite under millimetre-wave heating; the MMW record (≈10⁶–2×10⁷ W/m², 2–5 cm spots, ≥30 s) is melt-then-vaporise with incidental thermal cracking, and the only quantitative spall regimes are laser (1–5×10⁷ W/m², ≤0.5 s) and flame-jet (≤10⁷ W/m², modelled), so a 10 GHz spall-to-melt transition would be a new observation.

## Q11: When a beam melts granite or basalt, what governs the melt (ejected, pooled, glazed), how do reflectivity/emissivity/absorption change, is there plume/plasma shielding, and what melt depths and temperatures were measured?

### Takeaway
At 28 GHz the melt pools and sits (granite more viscous than basalt), is removed only when given a leak path or driven by vapour pressure into the walls, and becomes a more absorptive layer (28 GHz emissivity ≈0.66–0.70 molten, up to 0.91 with cavity trapping) whose temperature is capped near 2700–3000 °C by radiation (~4×10⁶ W/m²) and by air plasma breakdown above ~4 kW on a 32 mm beam; melt layers are ~4 mm thick and vitrify into a glass liner that thermally cracks. Laser glaze, by contrast, is reported to reflect or absorb successive pulses and stall the process.

### Cited Findings
- Melt rheology and pooling: "Basalt melts flowed much better than granite melts"; a 12 mm leak hole "was drilled into the specimen" so melt could leave; "If the waveguide was advancing and not stationary the borehole would be smaller" — [Woskov 2017, PSFC/JA-17-8](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/17ja/17ja008/17ja008_full.pdf).
- "The melted granite only filled about the central 1 11/16" (4.3 cm) diameter of the borehole. It did not flow to the walls"; the 25 mm basalt cube "mostly melt[ed] and flow occurred to a maximum of 3.75" (7 cm) in one dimension. The melt flow was helped by a waveguide flow of air collinear with the heating beam of about 490 scfh. A small layer of unmelted basalt about 4 mm thick under the visible melt" — [Woskov & Oglesby 2016, PSFC/RR-16-5](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/16rr/16rr005/16rr005_full.pdf).
- Melt depth: "the melted wall to be about 4 mm thick without any pressure behind the melt, an indication of beam wall absorption depth" after 41 min at 4.9 kW — [Woskov 2017](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/17ja/17ja008/17ja008_full.pdf). Basalt fill left "a thin weaker melt annulus about 1/16–1/8" (1–3 mm) thick" — [PSFC/RR-16-5](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/16rr/16rr005/16rr005_full.pdf).
- MMW emissivity (= absorbed fraction at 28 GHz, by calorimetry): "The 28 GHz emissivity generally decreases with temperature to a value near 0.7"; 0.91 inside the borehole "expected by power trapping inside the hole"; 0.70 at 10 cm standoff; 0.73–0.76 at 7.6 cm — [PSFC/RR-16-5](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/16rr/16rr005/16rr005_full.pdf). "MMW emissivity was measured to be ε_mm = 0.7 ± 0.1 for the rock melts at the highest temperatures" — [PSFC/JA-17-8](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/17ja/17ja008/17ja008_full.pdf). Molten granite 0.66 ± 0.03, rising to 0.70 ± 0.03 at vaporisation (2710 ± 120 °C); "the MMW emissivity of the molten granite is larger than in the infrared" — [Woskov & Michael 2012, J. Infrared Millim. THz Waves 33](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10762-011-9851-0) (abstract).
- Nonlinear absorption on melting: "Key insights are the nonlinear rise in temperature where the heated rock surface melts and forms the highly-absorptive melt layer" — [Houde et al. 2021](https://publications.mygeoenergynow.org/grc/1034509.pdf). Zhang's model encodes this as α = 14 Np/m solid → 56 Np/m liquid — [Zhang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.00681).
- IR emissivity of the melt (modelled, inferred from power balance): "the radiated power equals the absorbed power for an infrared emissivity less than the MMW emissivity" — [PSFC/JA-17-8](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/17ja/17ja008/17ja008_full.pdf); Zhang uses ε_IR = 0.7 (solid) and 0.3 (molten) and a tuned h = 56 W/m²K — [Zhang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.00681); the radiometer temperatures are corrected with 0.7 (local validation file).
- Radiative cap: "total losses, dominated by radiative heat transfer, are about 0.4 kW/cm² at ∼3000 °C" (4×10⁶ W/m²); "Maximum temperature was limited by radiative heat loss" — [PSFC/JA-17-8](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/17ja/17ja008/17ja008_full.pdf). Measured melt temperatures: 2500–3000 °C at 4.5 kW; 2710 ± 120 °C equilibrated vaporisation; Oglesby slab 2400–2870 K.
- Radiometer calibration bias: black-body calibration at the aperture "underestimates the actual peak surface temperatures"; a 1000 °C reading corresponds to ~1400 °C once ε = 0.7 is applied; "plotted temperatures are lower limits … could be as much as 40 to 50 % higher" — [PSFC/RR-16-5](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/16rr/16rr005/16rr005_full.pdf).
- Plasma/vapour shielding: power "generally limited to less than 4 kW due to plasma breakdown in high temperature, atmospheric pressure air"; "Sharp upward spikes … are interpreted as plasma breakdown events"; "The loss of directed energy with plasma breakdown prevented raising the melt temperature higher … and reemitted the heat to the surroundings eroding the wall"; no breakdown at 10 cm standoff with 490 scfh air purge — [PSFC/RR-16-5](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/16rr/16rr005/16rr005_full.pdf). Air purge up to 235 lpm "to control plasma breakdown" — [PSFC/JA-17-8](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/17ja/17ja008/17ja008_full.pdf). Vaporisation at 3.4 kW "before the final arc" — [Woskov & Michael 2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10762-011-9851-0). At 45 GHz/1.65×10⁷ W/m² "it is important to process the high-density plasma produced by the vaporized rock in time" — [Liu et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10762-022-00855-5). Pressure raises the breakdown threshold — [US 8,393,410](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8393410B2/en).
- Vapour pressure as the melt driver: face pressures "far exceed the injected purge pressure (5x increase for low-power)" — [Houde et al. 2021](https://publications.mygeoenergynow.org/grc/1034509.pdf); "volatilized creating a pressure to force the remaining melt material forward and into the sides making dense glassy walls" — [US 8,393,410](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8393410B2/en); field: "rock goop gets pushed into the walls of the hole before vitrifying into a glass-like lining" at 2 cm/min in a 10 cm bore at ~48 kW — [New Atlas 2025](https://newatlas.com/energy/quaise-energy-millimeter-wave-drill-demo-houston/) (press).
- Glass and cracking on cooling: "the basalt parent rock and the melt core plug both have many thermal stress fractures"; basalt melt cooled into "a black glass" — [PSFC/RR-16-5](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/16rr/16rr005/16rr005_full.pdf). Heat budget: 12.5 kJ/cm³ to heat granite to 3000 °C (incl. 0.9 kJ/cm³ fusion), ~14 kJ/cm³ vaporisation (estimate), melt threshold ~4.5 kJ/cm³ — [PSFC/JA-17-8](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/17ja/17ja008/17ja008_full.pdf).
- Microwave melt: basalt core melts by thermal runaway (conductor ~0.02 Ω m near 1100 °C), lava erupts through surface cracks at ~1 cm/s, forms obsidian, with a dusty plasma/fireball >1500 K and n_e ~10¹⁷–10¹⁸ m⁻³ — [Jerby et al. 2019, Sci. Rep. 9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6736850/); [Jerby 2004 poster](https://www.eng.tau.ac.il/~jerby/2004__Basalt_Jerby__Poster__4WC.pdf). Granite at 2.45 GHz melts with cracking at 6 kW/360 s (670 °C surface, thermal runaway) — [Lu et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00603-019-1743-y) (abstract).
- Laser glaze: "fused rock produced by laser irradiation reflects or absorbs successively irradiated laser beams, thereby refraining laser heat from being transmitted into the rock … causing the rock destruction process to cease completely" — [Laser destruction and treatment of rocks](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/253916443_Laser_destruction_and_treatment_of_rocks) (snippet); CO₂ on granite leaves "small glassy beads" and, in air on Inada granite, "transparent glassy objects crystallized on the walls" — [SPE 119914 / SPE 113177](https://onepetro.org/SPEDC/proceedings-abstract/09DC/09DC/SPE-119914-MS/147985) (snippets). Fibre-laser re-solidified zones carry ~50 µm gas-bubble pores; absorptance 54 % granite vs 89 % gabbro at 1064 nm — [Sci. Rep. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8758755/).

### Inferences
- A melt-layer model for the MMW case needs: (i) a viscosity law that distinguishes granite from basalt (pooling vs flow); (ii) an absorption jump on melting (≈4× in Zhang's α; ε_mm ≈0.66–0.70 vs higher cold-solid absorption measured by calorimetry); (iii) a radiative sink of ~4×10⁶ W/m² at 3000 °C (consistent with εσT⁴ ≈ 0.6×6.5×10⁶); (iv) a breakdown ceiling that, at 1 atm in hot air, sits near 4–5×10⁶ W/m² at 28 GHz for cm-scale spots; (v) a melt thickness of order the liquid 1/e depth (4 mm observed vs 18 mm from α_liquid = 56 Np/m, so conduction plus flow thin it).
- The surface-temperature plateau at 2700–2900 K in Oglesby's run is the radiation cap, not vaporisation; the vapour-front rule in the model should therefore not fire below ~3.2–3.5×10⁶ W/m² net absorbed flux unless the hole traps radiation.
- Laser and MMW glaze behave oppositely: laser glass reflects/stalls, MMW melt absorbs more, because the MMW skin depth collapses when the ionic conductivity of the melt rises.

### Gaps
- No direct measurement of cold-solid 28 GHz reflectivity of granite was found (Zhang assumes R = 0; Woskov's emissivity traces start high and fall with T but the solid-phase value is not quoted in the texts I opened). Woskov & Michael 2012 full text (the 130 GHz emissivity vs temperature curve, melt crater depth, calorimetric partition) is abstract-only.
- Melt depth was measured only on basalt walls (4 mm, 1–3 mm); no granite melt-pool depth or viscosity-temperature data at MMW conditions.
- Hartlieb's melt observations in granite (2.45 GHz) are second-hand via the arXiv review; the 2016 Minerals Engineering paper was not opened.
- Jerby microwave-drill melt behaviour in granite specifically was not found (his rock work is basalt).
- No quantitative plasma-plume attenuation (dB or absorbed fraction) is reported in any MMW source; only onset conditions and radiometer spikes.

### Verdict
MMW melt on granite pools rather than ejects, flows poorly compared with basalt, grows ~4 mm thick, absorbs more strongly than the solid (ε₂₈ GHz ≈0.66–0.70, up to 0.91 with trapping), and is capped near 2700–3000 °C by ~4×10⁶ W/m² of radiation and by air-plasma breakdown above ~4 kW on a 32 mm beam; it is removed only by a leak path or vapour pressure into the walls, where it vitrifies and thermally cracks.
