# Thermoelastic stress under a localised hot spot on an elastic half-space (Q10): surface vs volumetric heating, and the fracture mode each produces

## Q10a — Uniform (laterally infinite) surface heating of a half-space: is the in-plane stress purely local?

### Takeaway
Yes. For a traction-free half-space whose temperature depends on depth only, T = T(z,t), quasi-static uncoupled thermoelasticity gives σ_xx(z) = σ_yy(z) = −EαΔT(z)/(1−ν), σ_zz = σ_xz = σ_yz = 0, pointwise in z. There is no reference depth in the result: the stress at z is set by the local temperature alone, because the body is infinitely deep (zero mean-temperature and zero bending resultants) and laterally infinite (zero in-plane strain). I could not open the textbook pages themselves; the result is reconstructed below from the finite-plate formula given in Lu & Fleck (1998), from the biaxial-restraint formula as commonly stated, and from Welsh et al. (1988), who refer to it as the "uniform two-dimensional compressive stress" assumed in uniform-heating analyses.

### Cited Findings
- Free plate of half-thickness H with T = T(z,t): the plate "is free to expand with vanishing axial force ∫σ_xx dz = 0 and ∫σ_yy dz = 0 and vanishing normal stress in the through-thickness direction σ_zz = 0"; "the strain ε is independent of all spatial dimensions including z, and depends only on time t: the plate stretches uniformly but does not bend"; the thermal stress is then σ_xx(z,t) = σ_yy(z,t) = Eα[T̄(t) − T(z,t)]/(1−ν) where T̄ is the thickness-mean temperature (their eq. 3, isotropic reduction) — [Lu & Fleck 1998, *The thermal shock resistance of solids*, Acta Mater. 46(13):4755–4768, open PDF](https://www.fleckmech.org/papers/96.pdf).
- Under hot shock of such a plate, "the stress field is tensile at the centre of the plate" and compressive at the surface; under cold shock the surface is tensile and the centre compressive; "for all values of Bi, the maximum tensile stress is attained at the surface [cold shock] and the compressive stress is largest at the centre" — [Lu & Fleck 1998](https://www.fleckmech.org/papers/96.pdf).
- Biaxial restraint formula: "σ_x = σ_y = −EαΔT/(1−ν)", the factor 1/(1−ν) making biaxial restraint ≈ 43 % more severe than uniaxial for ν ≈ 0.3 — [MechCodex, Thermal Stress (teaching page; secondary source)](https://mechcodex.com/learn/strength-of-materials/thermal-stress).
- Welsh et al. (1988) note that earlier laser-heating analyses "assumed that the laser induces a uniform two-dimensional compressive stress" (i.e. the σ_rr = σ_θθ = −EαT/(1−ν) state appropriate to laterally uniform heating) "and may not have taken into account the effect of the zz component of elastic strain" — [Welsh, Tuchman & Herman 1988, J. Appl. Phys. 64:6274–6286](https://pubs.aip.org/aip/jap/article-abstract/64/11/6274/15663/) ([open PDF](http://www.columbia.edu/~iph1/Download/40.%20Welsh-Thermal%20Stresses%20and%20Strains%20Induced%20in%20Laser%20Processing%20-Journal%20of%20Applied%20Physics%2064,%206274%20(1988)%20doi.org.10.1063.1.342086.pdf)).
- The classical dynamic version of the uniformly heated half-space (sudden surface heating) is Danilovskaya (1950), "Thermal stresses in an elastic half space due to a sudden heating of its boundary", Prikl. Mat. Mekh. 14:316–318; the coupled transient half-space is Boley & Tolins 1962, J. Appl. Mech. 29:637–646 — citations listed in [EOLSS, Continuum Mechanics – Thermoelasticity (bibliography)](http://www.eolss.net/sample-chapters/c05/e6-161-11.pdf) and [arXiv 1507.06291 reference list](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.06291).

### Inferences
- Take Lu & Fleck's free-plate formula σ = Eα[T̄ − T(z)]/(1−ν) and let H → ∞ with a heated layer of finite depth δ ≪ H: T̄ = (1/2H)∫T dz → 0, so σ_xx(z) = σ_yy(z) = −EαΔT(z)/(1−ν) exactly, with σ_zz = 0 (traction-free surface + z-equilibrium with no z-variation of σ_zz). The ε_zz strain is free: ε_zz = (1+ν)αΔT/(1−ν). The half-space is simply the plate whose membrane and bending resultants vanish identically.
- **The "reference depth" the model uses is the finite-plate mean/bending term, which a half-space does not have.** A 1-D rule σ = E·[ε_th(surface) − ε_th(d_ref)]/(1−ν) reproduces the half-space result only when T(d_ref) = T0; whenever T(d_ref) > T0 (volumetric deposition, or a thermal penetration depth comparable to d_ref) it under-predicts compression and can even invert the sign while the true local stress is still compressive.
- Conditions: linear isotropic thermoelasticity, quasi-static (inertia negligible, true for heating times ≫ δ/c_sound), uncoupled, traction-free surface, laterally infinite heating (edge effects decay over ~ the heated-layer thickness), no residual stress, no plastic/damage softening.

**Verdict (Q10a): confirmed in form — σ_xx(z) = σ_yy(z) = −EαΔT(z)/(1−ν), σ_zz = 0, local in z with no reference depth; reconstructed from the open plate formula since the textbooks were not accessible.**

### Gaps
- Direct page citations in Boley & Weiner (*Theory of Thermal Stresses*, 1960), Timoshenko & Goodier (Art. 151, plate with T(z)), Nowacki, Parkus could not be fetched; the result is standard but is cited here through Lu & Fleck's equivalent plate statement.

## Q10b — Gaussian / circular hot spot of radius a on a half-space: published solutions and the stress distribution

### Takeaway
Steady-state hot spots on a half-space obey an exact theorem (Sternberg & McDowell 1957; Muki 1956): the stress is *planar and parallel to the surface* everywhere — σ_zz = σ_rz = σ_θz = 0 — and the in-plane stress is reduced relative to the 1-D scale: on the axis Welsh et al. (1988) find σ_rr = σ_θθ = −EαT_max/2, i.e. (1−ν)/2 ≈ 0.37 of S (ν = 0.25). Away from the axis σ_rr is set by the *mean* temperature inside r and σ_θθ = −EαT + EαT̄/2; a tensile hoop ring exists only where the surface temperature falls off faster than 1/r (transient or sharply bounded spots: up to +0.5 EαΔT for a top-hat, ≈ +0.11 EαT_max for a Gaussian temperature), and is absent in true steady conduction where T ∝ 1/r. Transient spots with δ ≪ a approach the 1-D biaxial state; the ratio δ/a (or, in steady state, 1 since δ ~ a) is what selects between S and ≈ S(1−ν)/2.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences (derivation from the planar-stress theorem; checked against Welsh eq. 26)
- With σ_zz = σ_rz = 0 everywhere (steady state), the exact 3-D in-plane compatibility equation at each depth is that of plane stress, so for a laterally decaying T: **σ_rr + σ_θθ = −EαΔT(r,z)** pointwise (Liouville: the 2-D-harmonic remainder vanishes). Radial equilibrium with σ_rz = 0 then gives **σ_rr(r,z) = −(Eα/2)·T̄(r,z)**, T̄(r) ≡ (2/r²)∫₀^r ΔT r' dr' (mean ΔT inside r at that depth), and **σ_θθ = −EαΔT + (Eα/2)·T̄**. At the axis T̄ = ΔT so σ_rr = σ_θθ = −EαΔT/2 — identical to Welsh's eq. (26), which validates the derivation. Relative to the 1-D scale S this is (1−ν)/2 = 0.40 / 0.375 / 0.35 for ν = 0.20 / 0.25 / 0.30.
- σ_rr is compressive everywhere (T̄ > 0). σ_θθ is tensile where ΔT(r) < T̄(r)/2, i.e. where the surface temperature drops faster than 1/r. Evaluated numerically (this note): (i) top-hat spot (ΔT uniform inside a): σ_θθ = −EαΔT/2 inside, jumps to **+EαΔT/2** just outside r = a, decaying as a²/r² — the exact analogue of the classical uniformly heated disc in an infinite plate; (ii) Gaussian *temperature* ΔT = T0 e^(−r²/a²): hoop tension starts at r = 1.12a, maximum **+0.109 EαT0 at r = 1.79a** (= 0.08 S for ν = 0.25); (iii) Lax's true steady surface profile for a Gaussian *flux*, T/T_max = e^(−ρ²/2) I0(ρ²/2) with ρ = r/w (decays as 1/r): σ_θθ stays **compressive at all r** (−0.24, −0.11, −0.05, −0.01 EαT_max at r/w = 1, 1.5, 2, 3) and σ_rr = −0.40, −0.32, −0.26, −0.18 EαT_max. Welsh's positive elastic ε_θθ beyond 1.4w is consistent with this (ε_θθ^el = (σ_θθ − νσ_rr)/E > 0 while σ_θθ < 0 because σ_rr is more compressive).
- Hence the tensile ring is a **transient / sharp-edge** feature: a conduction-steady spot on a half-space has none at the surface; a short pulse (heated skin δ ≪ a, temperature falling steeply at the beam edge) or a hard-edged beam does, bounded by +½EαΔT (plate-disc limit) and typically ~0.1 EαΔT for a soft Gaussian edge. This matches the silicon FE result (ring tension, radial cracks initiating at the spot edge).
- Scaling with δ/a. (a) Transient, δ ≪ a (thin hot skin under a wide spot): the interior of the spot is laterally constrained by its own cold substrate and neighbours → 1-D state σ_rr ≈ σ_θθ ≈ −S inside; σ_zz ≈ 0 to O(δ/a); the spot edge carries the hoop ring and a shear σ_rz ~ S·δ/a. (b) Steady or δ ≳ a: the hot volume is comparable in depth and width, the free surface relieves one constraint → axis stress −EαΔT/2 (= S(1−ν)/2), σ_zz = 0 exactly in steady state, σ_rz ≈ 0. Welsh's finite-absorption case (optical depth = w/15) shows the departure from the theorem is O(10⁻²) of σ_rr and peaks at z ≈ 0.5 w — the σ_zz that volumetric deposition introduces is small and lies at half a spot radius down.
- Magnitude bookkeeping for the model (fractions of S = EαΔT_max/(1−ν), ν = 0.25): axis compression 1.0 (δ ≪ a) → 0.375 (steady, δ ~ a); maximum hoop tension at the surface outside the spot 0 (steady Gaussian) to 0.08 (transient Gaussian) to 0.375 (hard edge); σ_zz ≤ ~0.01 (Welsh, finite absorption), zero in steady state.

**Verdict (Q10b): published half-space solutions (Sternberg–McDowell, Muki, Mindlin–Cheng, Barber, Welsh) give a planar stress state with σ_zz ≈ 0; the hot spot is in biaxial compression that drops from S (thin skin, δ ≪ a) to ≈ 0.37 S (steady, δ ~ a) on the axis, with a tensile hoop ring outside the spot only for transient/sharp-edged heating (≤ 0.5 EαΔT, ≈ 0.1 EαΔT for Gaussian), none in steady conduction.**

## Q10c — Volumetric (sub-surface-peaked) heating under a cooler free surface: sign of the surface stress, and published analyses

### Takeaway
For a half-space the local law of Q10a still applies to any T(z), monotonic or not: a surface that is cooler than the sub-surface peak but hotter than T0 is in **reduced biaxial compression**, −EαΔT_s/(1−ν), with the compression maximum at the temperature peak and σ_zz = 0 — it is **not** in tension. The familiar "cold skin in tension" of microwave-heated or radiation-heated bodies (Hasselman's thermal trapping, Hartlieb's basalt cylinders, glass under radiant heat) is a **finite-body** effect: the mean-temperature (free-expansion) term Eα T̄/(1−ν) of Lu & Fleck's plate formula puts the cooler surface in tension. That term vanishes for a half-space. Tension does arise at the grain scale (absorbing vs transparent minerals, Toifl, Jones) and on cooling (Yaseen); the laser volumetric-absorption half-space paper (Tayel 2020) exists but only its abstract was accessible.

### Inferences
- 1-D volumetric case (laterally wide beam, Beer–Lambert deposition, surface losses): σ_xx(z) = σ_yy(z) = −EαΔT(z)/(1−ν) for every z (Q10a). With ΔT_s < ΔT_peak the surface carries compression −EαΔT_s/(1−ν) and the peak carries −EαΔT_peak/(1−ν); σ_zz = 0. The model's rule gives E·α[T_s − T(d_ref)]/(1−ν) < 0 "drive" when T(d_ref) > T_s — a sign that is an artefact of the reference depth, not a physical tension.
- Finite-body contrast: for a free slab of thickness 2H with parabolic internal-heating profile, Lu & Fleck's formula gives σ(surface) = +(2/3)Eα(T_c − T_s)/(1−ν) (tension) and σ(centre) = −(1/3)Eα(T_c − T_s)/(1−ν). This is the "internally compressed, externally tensioned" state of the basalt cylinders and of Hasselman's thermally trapped plates; the tension is proportional to the mean-temperature term, which a semi-infinite borehole wall does not have. Rock below a drilled floor is finite only on the scale of the whole hole, not the spot.
- 3-D confined volumetric spot, steady: by the theorem σ_rr + σ_θθ = −EαΔT(r,z) at every depth; at the sub-surface peak the trace is most negative; the surface skin above it has trace −EαΔT_s < 0. Tension at the surface requires ΔT_s < T̄_s/2, i.e. a surface temperature profile that is *narrower* than its own mean — impossible for a profile whose maximum is on axis unless it decays faster than 1/r; for the steady Lax-type profile it does not happen (Q10b). So the surface stays compressive in both principal directions at the spot, and the only candidate tension is the hoop ring beyond the spot edge under transient conditions.
- A *tensile* surface over a volumetric spot therefore needs one of: (i) the surface actually cooled below T0 (e.g. gas-jet cooling of a previously hot floor, Yaseen's cooling phase), (ii) grain-scale mismatch (Toifl, Jones, Yang) which is independent of the macroscopic sign, or (iii) a finite body. None of these is the Beer–Lambert half-space geometry.
- On the laterally confined cold rock: it provides the constraint that makes the compression biaxial; it is the cold *surround* that experiences hoop tension (plate-disc analogue), and only transiently.

**Verdict (Q10c): a cooler-than-peak but warmer-than-ambient surface over a sub-surface temperature maximum in a half-space is in reduced biaxial compression, not tension; "cold skin in tension" results (Hasselman trapping, microwave cylinders, glass) are finite-body mean-temperature effects or grain-scale mismatch, so crazing/tensile surface cracking is not the predicted macroscopic mode for the MMW floor.**

## Q10d — Fracture mode produced by each stress state in brittle rock/ceramics, and how spot size enters

### Takeaway
A compressive hot skin over a cold substrate produces spallation by Preston's mechanism — flaws grow parallel to the surface under in-plane compression and the detached layer buckles and ejects — with a tensile zone *beneath* the skin, flake thickness tied to the heated depth, and flake lateral size smaller than the spot (mosaic); the spot sets how many flakes fit, not whether one forms, once a ≫ δ. The transient tensile hoop ring outside a small spot produces radial surface cracks (silicon, ceramics, laser-thermal cracking). Volumetric heating of finite bodies produces tensile cracking of the cooler exterior and grain-boundary cracking; cooling produces sub-surface tensile fracture. Hasselman's framework assigns surface-heated bodies to the crack-*initiation* (R, R') or heating-rate parameters, and small hot spots to the crack-*propagation*/energy-limited regime because the stored energy scales with the heated volume.

### Cited Findings
- Preston's mechanism as adopted for thermal spallation drilling: "1. Heating the rock produces large temperatures, and associated compressive-stresses, which are confined close to the surface due to the rock's low thermal conductivity. 2. The compressive stresses generate fractures parallel to the rock surface from pre-existing flaws. 3. Upon reaching a critical ratio of surface-area to width, the heated fragments adjacent to the surface buckle and are ejected as spalls." Dey & Kranz "employed plate-buckling theory to bound the heat flux required to generate spalls from a granite surface ... relating heat penetration depths to an assumed Weibull distribution of fracture-nucleation sites"; Rauenzahn & Tester (1989, IJRMMS 26(5):381–399) and Wilkinson & Tester combined this with jet fluid dynamics; spalls form "with maximum lengths several grain-diameters in extent but with thicknesses less than an average grain diameter"; grain-scale simulations suggest "analyses employing classical beam and plate buckling theory in a semi-infinite half-space may over-estimate the compressive stress required for spall production"; "the direction of fracture propagation is largely aligned with the local compressive stress"; asperities seed spalls, flat surfaces resist — [Walsh, Lomov & Roberts 2011, GRC Trans.](https://gdr.openei.org/files/174/WalshLomovRoberts_GRC_2011.pdf).
- Guo, Dyskin, Pasternak, Li & Huang (2024), Acta Geotechnica 19(8):5517–5543, flame heating of shale (899–1559 °C) with 3-D thermoelastic FE: "heating induces compressive stress in the surface layer and tensile stresses beneath it. The tensile stress is found to be sufficient to generate large tensile fractures ... the spallation compressive stress is sufficient to cause buckling of thin layers separated from the bulk of the rock"; spallation compressive stress "29 to 51 % of the uniaxial compressive strength"; the compressive/tensile ratio "increases with increasing spallation depth but [is] little affected by the flame temperature"; "the size of the buckling layer is smaller than the size of the spallation zone leading to a mosaic pattern"; tensile fractures develop perpendicular to the heated surface; higher flame temperature reduces depression diameter and depth — [UWA repository abstract](https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/publications/mechanics-of-thermal-spallation-and-fracturing-of-dry-rocks-produ/), [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11440-024-02260-9) (abstract only).
- Kant, Rossi, Madonna, Höser & Rudolf von Rohr (2017), "A theory on thermal spalling of rocks with a focus on thermal spallation drilling", JGR Solid Earth 122:1805–, DOI 10.1002/2016JB013800: model of spalling and thermal cracking and the operating conditions to initiate spalling; "with increasing uniaxial confining pressure, the measured spalling temperature decreases, as less additional thermal stresses are required to initiate spalling" — [ADS abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017JGRB..122.1805K/abstract) (abstract only).
- Spall geometry: "simulated spalls and collected field-test particles have size-dependent aspect ratios – exhibiting more equant shape at smaller sizes"; Rauenzahn & Tester calculated aspect ratio from plate and beam buckling — [Walsh et al., IJRMMS 2014 (abstract)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1365160914001324); failure-mode simulations: [Vogler, Walsh, von Rohr & Saar 2020, Acta Geotech.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11440-020-00927-7) (citation only).
- Laser spallation (Xu, Reed, Parker, Graves): "laser rock spallation ... utilizes laser-induced thermal stress to fracture the rock into small fragments before melting"; pulsing proposed so that "cyclic alternation between tensile and compressive stresses" aids spallation; patent: "short illumination by a single laser beam of irradiance about 1000 to 5000 W/cm² spalls rock and creates a hole as large as the beam spot size"; "firing the laser beam at the same spot for too long ... melted the rock and increased the specific energy. This implies that some relaxation time between laser bursts is needed"; mechanism "changed between spalling and melting by controlling either the specific power or exposure time" — [US 7,416,258 B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7416258B2/en), [Xu et al., Laser spallation of rocks for oil well drilling (RG)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/238107029_Laser_spallation_of_rocks_for_oil_well_drilling); Figueroa et al. 2002, "Rock removal using high-power lasers for petroleum exploitation purposes", High-Power Laser Ablation IV (citation only).
- Hot shock of a plate (surface compression, centre tension): "the centre of the plate is under tension and is prone to cracking; spalling of a surface layer due to large compressive stress is also a possibility"; an edge crack driven toward the compressive core "increasingly feels the presence of compressive stresses ... deflection ... parallel to the surface, resulting in crack branching or spalling" — [Lu & Fleck 1998](https://www.fleckmech.org/papers/96.pdf).
- Transient hoop ring → radial cracks: silicon ms-laser, "cracks are observed initiating at r = 0.5 mm [the hoop-stress peak] and extending along the radial direction" — [Appl. Opt. 2011](https://opg.optica.org/ao/abstract.cfm?uri=ao-50-21-3725); in thermal-shock ceramics "radial cracks are generated by tensile hoop stress", "radial stress is compressive while the tangential or hoop stress is tensile" — [UO₂ thermal-shock fracture, arXiv 2112.06645](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.06645).
- Hasselman parameters: "R, R′, R″ ... resistance to crack initiation; R‴, R″″, R_st ... crack propagation"; "one parameter represents the maximum allowable surface heating rate before fracture initiation"; the 1969 unified theory relates critical ΔT to crack length and density — [Hasselman 1970 compendium, Bull. - Microwave mode: grain-boundary tension at absorbing-phase boundaries (Toifl 2016), intergranular then transgranular cracks (Yang 2020), interior compression / exterior tension of cylinders (basalt, Energy 2023) — see Q10c citations.

### Inferences
- Mode map by stress state: (A) thin compressive skin, a ≫ δ → Preston spallation: surface-parallel flaw growth, buckling flake of thickness ~δ and width set by the Euler/plate criterion (hence the spot only needs to exceed the flake width; a ≫ flake → mosaic of flakes, Guo); tensile zone beneath the skin can add surface-normal cracks (Guo). (B) Steady/compact spot, δ ~ a → compression reduced to ~0.37 S, no surface tension; spallation threshold requires ~2.7× the surface ΔT of case A, favouring melting first (Xu patent: dwelling too long melts rather than spalls). (C) Transient sharp-edged spot → hoop ring outside the spot → radial cracks from the spot edge (silicon), a mode that removes no material by itself. (D) Volumetric finite body → exterior tension, grain-boundary cracking (microwave comminution literature). (E) Heat then cool → sub-surface tensile fracture (Yaseen).
- Spot-size entry: through δ/a (constraint, Q10b), through the number of buckling cells across the spot (Guo mosaic), and through stored elastic energy ∝ heated volume ~ a²δ, which is what Hasselman's propagation parameters (R‴, R″″) measure: small spots store little energy, so cracks arrest (stable) and flaking is incremental; large uniform heating stores enough for unstable propagation.
- For the MMW case (a ≈ cm, δ_abs 3–10 mm, surface cooled): the geometry sits between (A) and (B), with the compression peak sub-surface. The plausible mode is still mode (A)-type delamination, but with the parallel crack nucleating at or below the temperature peak rather than at the surface, and a thicker flake (~absorption depth) — this is inference; no source treats this configuration directly.

**Verdict (Q10d): compressive hot skin → Preston buckling spallation (flake thickness ~ heated depth, width ≪ spot, sub-skin tension secondary); transient spot-edge hoop tension → radial cracks; volumetric heating → exterior/grain-boundary tension only in finite bodies or at grain scale; spot size enters via δ/a constraint and stored energy (Hasselman propagation regime), not via the sign of the surface stress.**
