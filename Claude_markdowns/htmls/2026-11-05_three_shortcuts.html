Three skin shortcuts, and what replaces them

The removal chain runs heat → temperature → stress → onset → flake thickness → buckling check → removal. Three links were written for a flame, where the heated region is a thin skin. A beam heats a volume. This page shows each link as the code computes it today, the number it depends on, where the assumption fails, the alternatives, and which alternative removes a typed parameter rather than moving it.

Shortcut 1
Flake thickness = 0.4 × thermal skin depth
Shortcut 2
Surface temperature extrapolated from the top two cells
Shortcut 3
Stress = 0.531 × fully-constrained thermal stress

Reading the tags: typed a number set by hand or fitted once, rock a measured material property, derived follows from the fields and the properties with nothing left to set.

1 · The flake thickness and the 0.4

What the code computes

Each surface cell carries a line into the rock. From the top two cells' temperature rise above the initial state the code fits one exponential and reads its decay length ℓ, the thermal skin depth. The flake that leaves when the onset criterion fires has thickness

h_f = c_f · ℓ c_f = 0.4 (spall.flake_c_f) ℓ = dz / ln[(T_top − T0)/(T_1 − T0)]

The buckling check uses the same product as its plate thickness: a fired line is withdrawn unless C_L · c_f · ℓ ≤ L_span.

Where the 0.4 came from

The model card records it as a choice informed by literature, not a derivation: Wilkinson measured flakes of 0.1 to 1 mm, Rauenzahn's skin depths were 0.8 to 1.6 mm, so a ratio of 0.3 to 0.5 lands flakes in the observed range. It was then held fixed. Two things follow. It is a free parameter whose only justification is that the output looks right. And it is not independent: the buckling span was derived with it in place, so the pair (c_f, L_span) is one calibration. Change one and the other moves.

Why the depth scan cannot replace it

There is a depth scan that walks the line and finds how deep the fracture criterion holds. On every flame leg it returns the bisection floor, 3.9 µm on a 1 mm mesh, in 100 % of the uniform fixture's events and 61 % of the calibration deck's. The reason is physical, not a bug: the onset criterion fires at the instant the surface flaw just reaches critical, and at that instant the stress one step down is already below critical. The scan is an onset detector. Thickness is post-onset physics, how far the crack runs parallel to the surface before the flake frees itself, and no quasi-static scan at the onset instant can see it.

Where the shortcut fails under a beam

A beam at 28 GHz deposits over centimetres and a 10 GHz beam over tens of centimetres. The top two cells read nearly the same temperature, the fitted ℓ is huge or the code flags the column as flat, and the flake has no defined thickness. More deeply: ℓ was standing in for the depth of the compressed layer, and under volumetric heating that depth is not a temperature-gradient quantity.

C is the Hutchinson–Suo film argument. A layer of thickness t under in-plane compression σ stores elastic energy per unit area, and a delamination crack at depth t releases it. When the released energy reaches the fracture energy G_c the crack can run. With a stress that varies along the line:

G(t) = ∫₀ᵗ (1 − ν) σ(z)² / E(z) dz G_c = K_Ic² (1 − ν²) / E thickness = smallest t with G(t) ≥ G_c uniform σ, uniform E: t_min = K_Ic² (1 + ν) / σ² (E cancels)

Nothing in it is set by hand: the toughness and Poisson ratio are rock properties, the stress is the field. The buckling span then gives the other end: a layer thicker than L_span / C_L cannot buckle. Spall is possible only when the window is open, t_min ≤ L_span / C_L, which is the two-condition picture with both conditions derived.

Recommendation

Adopt C as the thickness and keep the buckling check as the upper bound. It deletes spall.flake_c_f, it is the same rule under a flame and a beam, and it predicts the two observed trends: thinner flakes at higher flux, and no flakes when the stress is low. The cost is honest: the flakes come out thinner than today's 0.4 ℓ by a factor of 3 to 5, so the removal rate per firing falls and the span calibration, which was tied to c_f, must be re-derived once. Whether the rate still closes Kant, Höser and Meier is then a test of the physics, not a knob. If it fails, that failure is a finding about the onset stress (section 3), because t_min scales as 1/σ².

2 · The surface temperature

What the code computes

The criterion fires on the surface temperature, but the mesh stores cell averages. From the top two cells the code fits the same exponential as above and extrapolates to the face:

x = ln[(T_top − T0)/(T_1 − T0)] = dz/ℓ T_s = T0 + (T_top − T0) · x / (1 − e^(−x)) flat : if the pair does not decay, x = 0, T_s = T_top

There is already a second route in the face list, used as the fallback when the pair is flat: the face temperature is solved from the face heat balance, flame heating in, radiation and convection out, conduction into the half-cell. That root is sign-agnostic and needs no profile shape.

Why it exists, and the question of not extrapolating

You asked whether it is better not to extrapolate. Figure 2 is the answer. The cell average under-reads the surface rise by the factor (1 − e−x)/x. At the production mesh, 1 mm cells on a 1.4 mm skin, that factor is 0.72: a 550 K surface rise reads as 396 K at the cell centre, 150 K low. Worse, the under-read depends on the mesh, so without a face value the onset temperature would move every time the mesh changed. P0 made the firing temperature mesh-independent to 0.1 to 0.4 % and that is what the extrapolation buys. Not extrapolating is only correct if the mesh resolves the skin, roughly ten cells across ℓ, which is 0.1 mm cells at the surface.

Push-back

Dropping the extrapolation outright would reintroduce a mesh-dependent onset of order 100 K at the production mesh, so I would not. But the two-cell fit is the weakest of the options because it infers the profile from two noisy numbers. The face heat balance is not a fit: it is the same energy balance the boundary condition already enforces, solved for the face temperature, and it is right for either direction of heat flow. D makes it primary and uses the exponential only as the sub-cell shape when heat is entering the rock. One refined-mesh twin at 0.1 mm settles which of A, B, D is closest, once, and that twin is the honest arbiter of mesh convergence for the whole chain.

3 · The stress and the 0.531

What the code computes

The one-dimensional law gives the surface stress as the fully constrained thermal stress times a relief factor:

σ = R · E(T_s) · [ε_th(T_s) − ε_th(T0)] / (1 − ν) R = 0.531294 R = UCS / σ_full(850 K) , UCS = 169 MPa → the criterion fires when σ_full reaches 318 MPa

R was set so that the fully constrained stress at a target onset of 850 K equals the rock's compressive strength; the 8 µm flaw then puts K_I at K_Ic at that stress. The model card already notes that R sits about 25 % below any physical relief estimate, "absorbing something that is not relief". It is a level knob with a physical name.

What the law assumes

Full in-plane constraint: a thin hot layer on a cold half-space cannot expand sideways, so all of its thermal strain becomes biaxial compression. That is a good description of a flame skin on a wide hot area. R then says only 53 % of that constraint is realised, and nothing in the law says why.

Recommendation

C first, as a diagnostic beside the law on the flame calibration deck. It costs one elastic solve every few steps and answers a question the project has carried since P2-2b: is the relief geometric or material? Only the answer decides whether R simply retires or is replaced by D. Either way it is not re-fitted, and under the beam the operator is the only form that sees the hot-spot geometry at all.

4 · How the three couple, and the order

The three are not independent, which is why they have to be decided together before the port:

  • The energy thickness in section 1 scales as 1/σ², so it inherits whatever section 3 decides about the stress level. With R in place the flakes come out 3 to 5 times thinner than today; with full constraint, 10 times thinner. The thickness question cannot be closed before the stress question.
  • The onset reads the surface temperature from section 2, and the stress law reads it too. A different face value moves the banked onset by a few kelvin.
  • The buckling span was derived with c_f in place. Deleting c_f re-derives the span once, from the same Kant brackets, with one fewer unknown.
