ALAMO · MMWSpalling · packet D2v · 24 September 2026

The Funnel

A convective boundary condition that never fell off with radius was quietly excavating the entire rock surface. Removing it turned a simulated borehole from a cone into a cylinder — and exposed an energy accounting hole that had been invisible for eleven packets.

This note describes one experiment in an ongoing programme to model thermal spallation drilling: boring through hard rock not with a mechanical bit but with a flame jet, which heats the surface fast enough that thermal stress flakes it away in millimetre-scale chips. The reference experiment is Thierry Meier's 2017 field trial at the Grimsel underground laboratory, where a 38 kW methane–oxygen burner drilled half a metre into granite in 1383 seconds.

The simulation is built in ALAMO, an adaptive-mesh finite-volume framework. It solves enthalpy-form heat conduction in the rock, computes the thermoelastic stress field, applies a Weibull-statistical spallation criterion, and removes material when that criterion fires. The question this packet answers is narrow and specific: does the model cut a hole of the right shape?

Part 1

Why shape, and not drilling rate

The obvious thing to score a drilling model against is its rate of penetration. We do not, and the reason is worth stating because it governs everything that follows.

An earlier packet in this programme found that the model reproduced Meier's drilling rate of roughly 1.5 m/h while simultaneously removing 1.9 times too much rock. Both statements were true at once: the model was cutting a hole that was too wide and advancing at about the right speed, so the volumetric error and the rate error happened to cancel in the one number being checked. The agreement was a coincidence of two compensating mistakes.

Rate is a single scalar, and a single scalar cannot tell you where a model is wrong. Shape can. So the score is the hole's diameter as a function of depth, Ø(z), compared against a profile digitised from Figure 8.8 of Meier's thesis — a photograph of the granite block sawn through the finished hole.

On the reference

The digitised profile is a visible width measured from a photograph, so it is a lower bound on the true diameter: the saw cut need not pass exactly through the hole's axis. Its precision is about ±5 mm. The thesis quotes a nominal 85 mm; the reported excavated volume of 3.42 litres over 0.5 m implies a mean nearer 93 mm. We therefore never quote agreement tighter than ±5 mm, and we treat the digitised curve as a shape reference rather than an absolute calibration.

Meier's hole is close to a cylinder: 96 mm at 10 mm depth, tapering gently to 85 mm at 200 mm. There is a mild collar near the mouth — about 10 mm of widening over the top 75 mm — but no funnel.

What the model was doing instead

The model was cutting a funnel: about 50 % too wide at the mouth, crossing the reference around 150 mm depth, and finishing far too narrow at 200 mm. This is a qualitatively different failure from being uniformly too wide or too narrow, and it points at something that acts strongly near the surface and weakly deep down.

A caution about an easy misreading here, because it cost us a whole packet. An earlier study reported that the model's minimum diameter tracks the burner's foot radius to within 2 mm — about 80 mm. From that we predicted the hole would come out around 80 mm and therefore too narrow. That was wrong, because the minimum diameter is the narrowest section of the hole, which sits down near the drilling front, and it is a different quantity from the diameter at a given shallow depth. The two coexist perfectly happily: the hole is 144 mm at the mouth and pinches to 66 mm at the bottom.

The cause: a boundary condition with no outer edge

The flame jet is represented not by solving the gas flow but by a convective boundary condition on the rock surface: a heat transfer coefficient h(r, s) and a gas temperature, where r is radius from the jet axis and s is the stand-off distance between the nozzle and the rock. The coefficient came from a wall-jet correlation — high under the impinging core, decaying outward as the jet spreads.

Evaluating that expression directly shows the problem:

1449W/m²K inside r = 18 mm
686at r = 40 mm
362at r = 76 mm
164at r = 168 mm, the domain corner

It decays roughly as 1/r and never reaches a floor. Combined with a patch radius set to 200 mm — larger than the entire computational domain — this meant that every column of rock in the simulation was receiving several hundred watts per square metre per kelvin of convective heating from a jet whose nozzle is 7.5 mm across.

Physically that is indefensible. Meier's burner is a body sitting inside the hole: an 80 mm slotted skirt resting on the rock, a water-cooled shield above it, exhaust venting up the annulus between the burner and the wall. Rock beyond about 40 mm radius is underneath the skirt or in that exhaust annulus. It sees grazing flow of order 40 W/m²K, not impingement of 362.

Why the collar formed early and then froze

Two further measurements pinned the mechanism down. First, the mouth diameter is final by 200 seconds and does not move for the remaining six minutes of the run: 141 mm at 200 s, 141 mm at 572 s. Second, the ring of rock between 50 and 76 mm radius recedes 33.6 mm by 300 s and then stops dead.

The explanation is a discontinuity in the model. The burner descends as it drills, and the code treats the nozzle plane as a hard switch: columns of rock below the plane receive the jet's heat transfer coefficient; columns above it receive exactly zero. Early in the run the whole rock surface is below the plane, so the whole surface is excavated at 160–360 W/m²K. As the burner sinks, the outer rock passes above the plane, its heating is cut to zero, and it stops. The funnel is carved in the first few minutes and then fossilised.

Part 3

The fix, and one trap inside it

The remedy is to give the burner a radially confined heat-transfer map that reflects it being a body inside a hole rather than a point source in free space. Three zones:

  • Impingement, r ≤ 40 mm: the existing correlation, unchanged. 40 mm is the skirt radius — this is the rock the jet actually strikes.
  • Annulus, blending to 142 W/m²K by 56 mm: the wall of the hole, seeing exhaust flowing up past it. The 142 comes from an area-expansion argument on the annulus flow, anchored to the measured mass flow; the defensible band from independent correlations is 81–250 W/m²K at a Reynolds number of 5568.
  • Outside, blending to zero by 76 mm: rock beyond the mouth, which in the real experiment sits under a sealed water-cooled wellhead.

Both blends are at least eight cells wide at the working mesh. That is not cosmetic. An earlier attempt at this same floor/wall split used a step in h and failed twice — once by jamming the burner, once by generating a 402 K lateral temperature step between adjacent columns that drove a numerical heat-leakage cascade. A separate packet established that the transition must be smooth over several cells. That is why this attempt was worth making: both of the earlier failure modes now have known remedies.

The trap: you cannot use the same map above and below the plane

The first draft of this packet applied one blended map everywhere. Checking the arithmetic before running showed that this would have decided the experiment by construction.

The impingement term clamps its stand-off argument to a minimum of 15 mm. For a column above the nozzle plane the geometric stand-off is negative, so the clamp returns the strongest near-field value. Blended at the wall radius, an above-plane column at 42–47 mm would have received:

The floor of the hole receives about 520 kW/m². The wall would have received up to 483 — roughly a metre per hour of lateral recession — and the hole would have been driven out to wherever the blend happened to hand over, regardless of any physics.

The fix is to branch on the stand-off inside the expression: below the plane the blend, above the plane a flat annulus value. That is the dashed violet line in Figure 1. It is the difference between a boundary condition that models a wall and one that manufactures a diameter.

Part 4

The result

Three runs, identical except for the listed changes, on a 2 mm mesh over about 400 seconds of drilling.

M0the existing map, untouched
M1+ confined map, no above-plane heating
M2+ above-plane wall heating

Before anything is attributed, M0 has to be provably the same run as the frozen reference it is compared against. It is: M0's removal-event log and its integrated-diagnostics file are byte-identical to the archived run, 114 599 event rows and 4 184 diagnostic rows. Any difference between M1 and M0 is therefore caused by the listed keys and nothing else.

The scored criterion is the mean absolute difference from the reference at 10, 25 and 50 mm depth — the depths where the funnel lives. It falls from 37.2 mm to 3.8 mm, against a bar of 20 mm fixed and hashed before the runs. M1 tracks the reference within 4.4 mm at the mouth and 0.2 mm at 200 mm depth.

The confinement also works exactly where it was built to work. Mean recession of rock beyond 76 mm radius — the rock that should never be touched — is 0.000 mm on M1, against 0.216 mm on M0. And a pre-launch thermal trace confirmed the compiled boundary condition really is the intended one: over the 2463 columns beyond 76 mm, the maximum temperature rise was 0.013 K, where the old map ran 362 W/m²K.

Part 5

M2, and the hole in the energy budget

The third run was meant to test a separate mechanism. A previous packet had established that heating the exposed vertical faces of rock above the nozzle plane is what makes the hole's diameter converge under mesh refinement. M2 turns that on. It was expected to widen the hole modestly.

It widened it by 37 to 41 mm at every depth and stripped 76.7 mm of rock from the 50–76 mm ring, against M1's 0.5 mm.

The cause is a genuine conservation defect, found during this packet and not anticipated by it. The jet's energy is tracked by an enthalpy march: the gas starts hot at the nozzle, is binned by radius, gives up heat to each ring of rock in turn, and the accumulated transfer is capped by the gas's own enthalpy. That march contains two loops, and both skip columns above the nozzle plane.

Why this was invisible

Skipping above-plane columns is what allows a prescribed above-plane gas temperature to survive the march at all — so the skip looks like a feature. But it also means heat delivered above the plane is never deducted from the jet's enthalpy budget. The march's internal invariant (power to the face must not exceed the enthalpy cap) covers below-plane columns only. The global energy ledger cannot catch it either, because the flux is counted there — the ledger closes to 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁴, as it should. Energy is conserved domain-wide; it is the jet that is being over-drawn.

So M2's above-plane heat source is limited only by the radial confinement, never by the gas actually having that energy to give. The 2.4 kW it delivers is free. This must be fixed before any packet widens the annulus band, and it makes M2's result uninterpretable as physics — though entirely interpretable as a diagnostic, which is how it is being used.

Part 6

What this does and does not establish

The result is strong enough that the caveats matter more than usual.

The diameter is substantially an input, not a prediction

M1's wall sits at about 44 mm radius, which is inside the 40–56 mm blend. The hole's radius is therefore largely set by where that blend hands over. The radii were chosen from the burner's engineering drawing — the 40 mm skirt, the 76 mm measured mouth ring — and not from Meier's hole, so this is not tuning. But it means the model is being told roughly where the wall should be, and it is putting it there. The honest statement is that the funnel has been removed down to the geometry that was supplied, not that the hole diameter has been predicted.

This is one mesh

Everything here is a 2 mm calculation. A previous packet showed that the above-plane heating mechanism reveals itself as a difference between meshes, which a single mesh cannot see by construction. No convergence claim is made, and none can be. The 1 mm run is the next expensive step.

The model still drills about twice too fast

M1 cuts 362 mm in 402 seconds — 3.2 m/h, against Meier's 1.3–1.6. This is inherited from the baseline configuration, which is a known runaway case, and is not caused by the confinement; M0 runs at 2.5 m/h. But it means the excavated volume (M1 at 0.82× the reference, pro-rata) is not a meaningful comparison either, and the rate will have to be confronted even though this packet deliberately did not score it.

One number in the map is still underived

The factor that scales heating on exposed vertical faces is set to 0.2. It has never been derived from anything; it was introduced as a placeholder, and it sets the magnitude of the wall heating. It is frozen and unscanned in this packet, which keeps the comparison clean, but it is a genuine loose end and it should be retired by derivation rather than by fitting.

A prediction that was refuted, and in which direction

Before the runs we pre-registered that the 16 mm blend imposes a numerical floor of about 96–100 mm on the achievable diameter, with the prediction counting as refuted if any leg cut below 92 mm at shallow depth. M1 cut 91.69 mm at 50 mm depth. Refuted — narrowly, and in the favourable direction. The floor argument was correct in form but about 5 mm pessimistic.

We record this because the discipline is the point. Predictions are written down with explicit refutation thresholds and hashed before the runs start, so that a result cannot be reinterpreted after the fact. In this programme that practice has already caught a case where an earlier packet's author predicted their own mechanism would fail, was refuted by their own stated test, and reported it.

Part 7

Where this goes

Three things follow, in order.

Fix the enthalpy accounting. Above-plane heat has to be drawn from the same budget as below-plane heat. Until then M2's number is a diagnostic, not a measurement, and no packet can responsibly widen the annulus band.

Run the fine mesh. A 1 mm calculation over the same window costs roughly three hours. It settles whether the near-cylinder survives refinement, and it looks specifically at the wall, where an earlier packet established that the vertical profile does not converge even where the diameter does.

Then confront the rate. With shape under control and a converged metric, the factor-of-two in penetration rate becomes the next well-posed question rather than a confounded one.

The methodological point

Eleven packets in this line failed or were withdrawn before this one passed. Four of them were chasing a residual mesh discrepancy that turned out to be the abrupt edge of a prescribed boundary condition rather than a physical mechanism at all; two more were withdrawn before launch when pre-flight arithmetic showed they would have decided their own result by construction. What eventually worked was not a better model but a better metric: replacing a single averaged rate with a spatially resolved profile, which immediately said where the model was wrong. Every packet since has been cheaper and more decisive than the ones before it.
