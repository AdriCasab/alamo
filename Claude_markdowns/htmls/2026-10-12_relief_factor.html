Where The Relief Factor Went

The model has been carrying a 25 % discrepancy in its relief factor for two weeks. It now looks like a borrowed toughness table, not a missing mechanism. Along the way, four versions of the plan turned out to rest on arithmetic that did not hold.

12 October 2026No simulations runAll numbers computed from the model's own property tables

Everything below is a desk result. Nothing here comes from a simulation. The numbers are produced by solving the model's firing condition by hand, using the same property tables the code uses. That is worth saying plainly, because a desk result can be checked but it cannot be trusted the way a run can.

Part 1What the relief factor is, and why it was a problem

When a flake is about to lift off the rock face, not all of the stress in the heated skin is available to drive the crack. Some of it is relieved — by microcracks, by the flake's edges already being free, by the surface bulging. The model collects all of that into one number between zero and one, which multiplies the stress before the crack sees it. Call it the relief factor.

It should be possible to say roughly what that number ought to be. The flake fails in compression, so the stress that matters is measured against the rock's crushing strength. Relief is therefore strength divided by stress. For Grimsel granite that gives a band, because the crushing strength is known to about plus or minus twenty per cent.

The problem: when the number was fitted, so that the model fired at the temperature Kant measured, it came out about 25 % below that band — and under both treatments of thermal expansion. A relief factor below its own band means the model needs the stress pulled below the rock's crushing strength before it will fire. Something was being absorbed that is not relief.

Part 2The result: stress cancels, and one number is left

The useful step was to stop fitting the relief factor and instead set it to what it means — strength over stress. Put that into the firing condition and the stress cancels:

The firing condition, with relief set to its definition
\[ R\,\sigma\,\cdot\,1.99\sqrt{a} \;=\; K_{Ic}(T_s), \qquad R \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{UCS}}{\sigma} \] \[ \Longrightarrow\quad K_{Ic}^{\,\mathrm{needed}} \;=\; 1.99\,\mathrm{UCS}\,\sqrt{a} \;=\; 0.951\ \mathrm{MPa}\sqrt{\mathrm{m}} \]
  • \(R\)relief factor, dimensionless, 0 to 1
  • \(\sigma\)thermal stress in the heated skin, MPa
  • \(\mathrm{UCS}\)crushing strength, 169 ± 37 MPa for Grimsel granite
  • \(a\)flaw half-length, held at 8 µm in the deck
  • \(K_{Ic}\)the rock's toughness at the surface temperature
  • \(1.99\)the edge-crack geometry factor — Equation 3 of the plan. Re-derived here from the weight-function integral in the code rather than quoted: \(2\!\int_0^{\pi/2}\! g(\sin\theta)\,d\theta/\sqrt{\pi} = 1.9891\)

Stiffness, thermal expansion and Poisson's ratio all drop out. What is left is a single question: at what temperature does \(K_{Ic}(T_s)\) fall to \(0.951\)? That is a much cleaner question than the one we were asking, and it needs no fitting at all.

Part 3The likely culprit: a toughness table from the wrong rock

The model takes its toughness-versus-temperature curve from Nasseri's measurements. Those were made on Westerly granite. Our rock is Grimsel. And Grimsel's own curve is already sitting in the code — it was added with the other material properties — but the firing criterion cannot reach it. The input key that selects a toughness law accepts only two values, and the Grimsel option is not one of them.

The two curves disagree where it matters:

The result

Switching to Grimsel's own toughness curve closes the 25 % relief discrepancy to between 2 % and 5 %. The fix is one extra option on an input key and one branch at each of the two places the criterion reads toughness.

With that change, and nothing fitted, the model fires at 839 to 845 K against a target of 850 K, and the flaw it implies is 7.7 µm against the 8 µm the deck holds. For comparison, the old Westerly table at the same relief fires at 734 K — over a hundred kelvin away. So the two curves are easy to tell apart, which means the test has real power.

Part 4What failed

Four successive versions of the work package were written, reviewed, and found to rest on arithmetic that did not hold. They are listed here because each failure changed what the step is for, and because the pattern matters more than the individual mistakes.

  1. Separating toughness from relief is impossible The plan was to measure relief and a toughness correction separately, using two firing temperatures. But both are plain constants, one on each side of the firing condition, so only their ratio ever appears. Two measurements give that ratio twice. They never give the two numbers. Four versions of the plan were built on this.
  2. The flux-exponent formula was wrong three times Kant measured how the firing temperature moves with heat flux. A formula was written to turn that measurement into the flaw-size exponent. The first version left out the fact that toughness falls as the rock heats. The second left out the fact that stress does not rise in proportion to temperature. Each correction changed the answer by a factor, not a few per cent. The formula was never needed: the model computes each firing temperature itself, so the slope can simply be measured.
  3. A 34 % shortfall that was not there The gap between what the new toughness curve supplies and what is needed was reported as 34 %. It was two different temperatures divided by each other. At one consistent temperature the gap is 2 to 5 %. Left uncorrected, this would have sent the next step looking for a mechanism that does not need to exist.
  4. The firing-temperature test is a cliff, not a measurement Because stress cancels, the test depends only on crushing strength and flaw size. Both are uncertain. One standard deviation of crushing strength is the difference between never firing at all and firing 130 K early. A five per cent band on the firing temperature looks precise and is not.
  5. The two property treatments were physically incoherent To bracket the uncertainty in thermal expansion, one treatment capped expansion above 300 °C — on the grounds that the extra expansion goes into microcracks rather than loading the flake. But the same microcracking also softens the rock, and that softening was left at full strength. Capping one and not the other was the only combination that appeared to reproduce Kant's flux measurement. Treated coherently, the model misses it by a factor of two to three.

Part 5The deeper problem: we are working at the edge of the data

Grimsel's measured property tables stop at 600 °C. Both the stiffness curve and the toughness curve repeat their 600 °C value at 650 °C, and the code holds them flat above that. The model fires at 566 to 577 °C. So every slope we quote rests on the last measured interval, 550 to 600 °C, and anything evaluated above 600 °C is reading a clamp rather than a measurement.

That has two consequences, and the second is the one that matters.

First, some numbers in the earlier analysis — including the relief factor fitted during the previous step — were evaluated at 608 °C, past the last data point. They are reported now as context, not as results.

Second, and more seriously: in that final interval the stiffness loss and the expansion gain nearly cancel each other out.

Why this limits the flux exponent

Stress is nearly flat where the rock fires, because two larger effects are cancelling. The flux exponent depends on the slope of stress with temperature — which is that small residual. Thermal expansion above 300 °C is the one property the reference marks as an estimate, good to about plus or minus thirty per cent. An error that size does not nudge the residual; it can reverse its sign.

This is not bad luck. We fire in the quartz transition, which is exactly where softening and expansion trade off against one another — and exactly where the measurements stop.

So the flux exponent cannot carry quantitative weight. It can still say whether the model produces any flux dependence at all, whether the sign is right, and whether the size is within a factor. It cannot be compared digit for digit with Kant's measurement.

The flaw size implied by the firing condition is immune to all of this, because it depends only on toughness and crushing strength — no stress, no stiffness, no expansion. That is why it, and not the firing temperature, is now the result the step is judged on.

Part 6One smaller finding, from the mesh side

The flaw size is scaled by the volume of rock under stress, divided by a reference volume. Until now that reference has been set to the size of a computational cell, which makes the whole term cancel exactly — which is deliberate, because it keeps the answer independent of the mesh.

The planned change makes the stressed volume physical: the depth the heat has actually reached. Once that happens the reference volume stops cancelling and becomes a live scale — and the two test cases use cells of different size, so the same physics would make the flaw larger on one and smaller on the other, by up to a factor of 1.7. One input key cannot be both a mesh anchor and a material constant. It has to be split, and the new one anchored where the scaling factor is exactly one, which is the only choice that does not make the flaw depend on the exponent being measured.

Part 7What this means for the programme

Retired

Fitting relief and a toughness correction together. Fitting the flaw-size exponent to Kant's flux measurement. The formula relating the two. Capping expansion without also softening stiffness. The claim that a third of the discrepancy is unexplained.

Standing

Relief computed from strength over stress, not fitted. The flaw-size exponent taken from published granite measurements rather than fitted. Grimsel's own toughness curve made reachable. The implied flaw as the quantity the step is judged on.

The step that follows has no fitted parameters in it, which is the first time that has been true in this part of the programme. Relief comes from a definition, the exponent from the literature, the toughness from the rock's own table. Three quantities are then predictions rather than outputs of a fit: the firing temperature, the implied flaw, and the flux exponent.

Whether that is convincing depends on something outside the model. The step is being set up so that a near miss and a clean hit are distinguishable in advance, because a prediction that lands within a couple of per cent on a model with this much property uncertainty should be treated as suspicious until the sensitivity has been read. Tester's group said the same thing about their own matched results in 1993: good agreement can be the result of errors cancelling.

The real constraint

Measured stiffness and toughness for Grimsel granite above 600 °C. Without them, no formula can carry the flux exponent, because the slope it needs lives in the interval where the tables end. This is a data problem, not a modelling one.

Second: the published split between mechanically measured and spallation-inferred Weibull moduli — 20 against 8 to 13 — is the number the literature comparison rests on, and it has been taken second-hand from a paper that was never obtained. It should be bought before any of this is written up.
