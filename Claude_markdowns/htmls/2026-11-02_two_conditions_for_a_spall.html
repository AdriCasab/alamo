ALAMO · MMWSpalling · 2026-11-02 · a reading of P2-14a and the literature study

Two conditions for a spall, not one blended drive

Why the constraint depth produces an onset that climbs toward the minimum flux, why no value of it can avoid that, and how the same two goals are met by keeping the stress local and adding a separate thickness condition. Every figure is computed on this page from the equations shown; the measured bands and the P2-14a numbers are overlaid as they stand on disk.

1What the constraint depth was asked to do

The direction document gave the constraint depth d two jobs, and both are legitimate:

  1. A minimum heat flux. A face that is slowly soaked should stop spalling. Kant measured this on Gotthard and Bethel granite: spalls at 130 mm standoff, none at 140 to 160 mm, which is a flux of roughly 0.3 to 0.5 MW/m².
  2. A meaning under a beam. Under volumetric deposition the surface flux is not the surface gradient, so a rule written on the temperature profile is needed. Reading the profile is right; the question is what to read from it.

It does both by changing the stress formula. The drive at a depth z becomes the temperature difference between that depth and a point d further in:

the constraint-depth drive (P2-2e, ported to the face list in P2-14a) σd(z) = R · E(Tz) · ∫T(z+d)T(z) α(θ) dθ / (1 − ν)

When the heated layer is thick, the point at z + d is already warm, the integral is short, and the stress is small. That is how the minimum flux appears. It is also, as the next sections show, how the onset climb appears, and the two cannot be separated because they are the same term.

2The stress in heated rock is local

For a body that is wide compared with the heated region, with a free surface, the in-plane thermal stress at any depth depends only on the temperature at that depth. The hot rock is compressed because it wants to expand and the cold rock beside it will not move. There is no reference depth in this result, and it holds for surface heating and volumetric heating alike (Timoshenko and Goodier; Lu and Fleck 1998 in the limit of a thick plate).

half-space, free surface, temperature varying with depth only; compression negative σxx(z) = σyy(z) = − E · β · [T(z) − T0] / (1 − ν), σzz = 0

Figure 1 draws two temperature profiles and the stress each produces. The left profile is a flame: a thin hot skin. The right is a beam with surface losses: the peak sits a few millimetres down. In both the stress is compressive wherever the rock is hot. The dashed curves are what the constraint-depth construction reads instead: the difference between T(z) and T(z + 2 mm). Under the flame it reads a fraction of the true rise. Under the beam it reads a negative number at the surface, and the model concludes there is no drive at all.

3What the constraint depth does to the onset against flux

Take the simplest case that can be solved exactly: constant properties, a semi-infinite body, a constant surface flux q. The temperature rise is

constant flux on a half-space (Carslaw and Jaeger §2.9) T(z,t) − T0 = (2q/k) · √(κt) · ierfc( z / 2√(κt) ), ierfc(x) = e−x²/√π − x·erfc(x)

The constraint-depth drive at the surface is D(t) = T(0,t) − T(d,t). It grows with time but saturates: as the gradient becomes steady, D → q·d/k. Spalling requires D ≥ ΔTc, the critical rise that the stress criterion needs at the flaw. So a minimum flux exists, exactly as intended:

the minimum flux the construction produces qmin = k · ΔTc / d

Figure 2 asks the next question: at what surface temperature does the face fire when the flux is above that minimum? Move the slider and watch the curve. For any d, the onset is flat at high flux and climbs steeply as the flux approaches qmin. The measured bands sit under it as shaded regions, and the P2-14a production-form results from the calibration deck are the dots.

4Why no value of d can remove the climb

Divide the drive equation through by ΔTc and write the flux as a multiple of the minimum, Q = q/qmin. The time to fire and the surface temperature at firing then depend only on Q. Every parameter, d, k, κ, ΔTc, drops out:

universal form, constant properties; τ = d / 2√(κt) at the moment of firing fire when (Q/τ) · [ 1/√π − ierfc(τ) ] = 1, then Ts,fire − T0 = ΔTc · Q / (√π · τ)

Figure 3 plots that single curve. It is the shape of the constraint-depth law, independent of the number chosen for d. Flatness within ten per cent needs a flux at least twice the minimum. At one and a half times the minimum the face fires 40 per cent hotter than the flat level. At one and a quarter times, twice as hot.

This is the sense in which the problem is the form and not the value. Re-fitting d slides Figure 2 left or right along the flux axis. It cannot flatten it. Kant's own data walk down to within about 1.3 times his threshold and stay flat, which the curve says is impossible for this construction.

A note on the binary. P2-2e found that the binary needs d = 1.5 to 2.0 mm to reproduce Kant's bracket, where the offline constant-property calculation needed 7 to 10 mm. The difference is the temperature dependence of the Grimsel tables (expansion triples between 300 and 500 °C). That changes the number, not the shape: the P2-14a series climbed by 122 K over a factor 4.7 in flux, in line with Figure 3.

5The two conditions the literature actually uses

The only derived minimum flux in the spallation literature does not touch the stress. It is Preston's buckling idea, made quantitative by Dey and Kranz and carried by Rauenzahn and Tester as their Equations 15 and 16. A compressed layer over a cold substrate can only pop out as a flake if it is thin compared with the span of the flaw it detaches along. A thick layer under the same stress stays put.

Condition A: the stress at the surface flaw (unchanged, d = 0)

the existing criterion, referenced to the initial temperature Sp = KI / KIc(Ts) ≥ 1, KI = 1.12 · σ(Ts) · √(π a), σ(Ts) = R · E(Ts) · ∫T0Ts α dθ / (1 − ν)

This fixes a critical surface temperature from the rock alone. It is flat with flux by construction, which is what the data show. On Grimsel it is the banked 843.76 K.

Condition B: the layer must be thin enough to buckle

A plate of thickness x under biaxial compression σ buckles over a span L when σ exceeds the plate's critical stress. With σ set by the thermal strain, the condition becomes a pure aspect ratio:

Rauenzahn and Tester 1989, Eq. 16, after Preston and Dey and Kranz L / x ≥ CL, CL = π / √( 6 (1 + ν) · β·ΔTs )

For Grimsel at onset the strain integral is about 7.6 × 10⁻³, which gives CL ≈ 13: a flake must be at least thirteen times wider than it is thick. The flake thickness scales with the heated layer. The model already carries that rule as x = cf·δ with cf = 0.4, and the heated-layer depth is the surface rise divided by the surface gradient:

heated-layer depth and the resulting minimum flux δ = ΔTs / (dT/dz)surface = k · ΔTs / q (flame), x = cf·δ, spall possible iff Lmax ≥ CL · cf · k·ΔTs / q
⇒ qmin = CL · cf · k · ΔTs / Lmax

Here Lmax is the largest span a flake can bridge: the lateral size of the flaw population, observed as spall diameters of ten to twenty grains, one to a few centimetres. Figure 4 shows the minimum flux this gives against that span, with Kant's measured band. Spans of 1.4 to 2.3 cm reproduce his 0.33 to 0.5 MW/m² with no free parameter beyond the span itself.

6What this says about the beam, and about your 10 GHz observation

Under volumetric deposition the layer depth is no longer kΔT/q. It is read from the profile and is at least the absorption depth. Condition A is unchanged: the surface is compressed in proportion to its own temperature rise, so the onset is reachable. Condition B then asks whether a flaw spans thirteen times a flake that is now thick. If the absorption depth is a few millimetres, centimetre spans suffice and the model predicts thick flakes delaminating near the depth where the compression falls off. If it is centimetres, no flaw in a granite spans the required tens of centimetres and the brittle law switches itself off; the surface then goes on to the solidus. That is a regime boundary set by absorption depth, which the constraint depth could not express because it answered "no spall" regardless.

The 10 GHz case is probably not a half-space. The literature study found one room-temperature measurement of granite absorption at 28 GHz (Woskov, Barre granite), giving a power penetration depth of about 36 mm. Loss of this type scales roughly with frequency, so at 10 GHz the cold penetration depth is of order 100 mm, falling to perhaps 20 mm when hot. A laboratory block of ten centimetres is then heated nearly through. In a finite body the stress is no longer local: it is referenced to the mean temperature of the body, and a surface that is cooler than the mean, because it loses heat to the air, is in tension.
free plate of thickness H heated through its depth (Lu and Fleck 1998); T̄ is the mean over the thickness σxx(z) = E · β · [ T̄ − T(z) ] / (1 − ν) ⇒ a surface with T(z=0) < T̄ is in tension

So the observation that granite spalls at low intensity and melts at high intensity is consistent with the two-condition picture in either geometry, but the mechanism differs, and so does what to measure:

  • Block size against penetration depth. If the block is smaller than about three penetration depths, you are in the right-hand panel and the flame onset data do not apply directly.
  • Where the flakes form and how thick they are. Buckling flakes are wide and thin, inside the spot, and leave a compressive-layer thickness. Tensile exfoliation produces cracks at the surface first, often at the spot edge, and flakes that follow cracks.
  • Time to first spall against intensity, and whether it happens after the beam is off. Spalling after shut-off is a finite-body signature: the surface cools fastest and goes further into tension.
  • Transmission or reflected power during heating. The literature says the sharp absorption event is the melt, not a solid-state runaway. A trace through a slab would show whether that holds at 10 GHz.

7What the two conditions change in the model, and what they do not

UnchangedCondition A is today's criterion at d = 0: the Grimsel tables, the 8 µm flaw, the relief factor 0.531, the banked 843.76 K. The flat onset the data show is already in it.
RetiredThe reference depth inside the stress formula. Its two goals survive; its mechanism does not, because that mechanism is the climb.
AddedCondition B: fire only if a flaw of span L ≥ CL·x exists, with x the flake thickness from the heated-layer depth read off the temperature profile, so it works for any heat source. One new rock property, the span distribution Lmax, in metres.
Made explicitThe flake thickness rule x = 0.4 δ with its 1.5 mm cap. The cap equals kΔTs/q at about 1.2 MW/m² and has been acting as a hidden minimum-flux rule. Under Condition B it is no longer needed for that purpose and should be tested on its own.

Checks that would confirm or refute this on the existing binary

  1. Kant's five standoffs at d = 0 with Condition B. Flat onset at 130 mm and above, silence at 140 to 160 mm, for one span value. Compare the span found with the spall diameters in his photographs.
  2. The calibration-deck flux series. Flat within the Weibull scatter from 0.34 to 1.6 MW/m², and silent at 0.12 MW/m² for a span under about 5 cm.
  3. The beam columns of P2-2e. At 1/α = 0.5 mm the behaviour should be flame-like. At 1/α = 10 mm the condition should demand a span of order 5 cm, so it should switch off and let the column melt. Both are thirty-second runs.
  4. Höser at 15 s. No volume loss relative to today's d = 0 run on the floor, and the walls governed by the span rule rather than by a hotter reference.

What remains uncertain

  • The Dey and Kranz paper with the numerical form of the buckling bound was not obtainable; the constant 13 comes from Rauenzahn and Tester's expression and the Grimsel strain integral.
  • The span Lmax is a lateral flaw size of centimetres. The Weibull draw in the code is a crack depth of micrometres. They are different populations and the first has never been measured on Grimsel.
  • The thickness rule x = 0.4 δ has never been checked against a measured Grimsel flake. Höser's 200 to 300 µm figure is a fragment-size peak, not a thickness.
  • Under a beam the right definition of δ from a profile with an interior peak needs choosing and testing; the natural choice is the depth where the compression has fallen to a fixed fraction of its surface value.
