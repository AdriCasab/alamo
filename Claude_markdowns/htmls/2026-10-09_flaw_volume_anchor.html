The Flaw Volume Anchor

Our model wants a Weibull modulus of 3–5. Every published granite value is 10–25. That gap looked like a defect in the fit — until it turned out Wilkinson & Tester measured both numbers in 1993 and reported the same discrepancy.

The anomaly
Kant's measured flux exponent forces m far below every published granite modulus.
The resolution
Published values are strength moduli. Converted, our chain demands 6.8–10.1 — which brackets Wilkinson & Tester's spallation-inferred 8 for Barre granite.
The fix still needed
Split weibull.V0 into a mesh anchor and a material reference, anchored where the size factor is exactly 1.

The literature already documents our anomaly

Wilkinson & Tester obtained the Weibull modulus for granite two different ways and got two different answers:

Published granite moduli
how it was obtainedm
Mechanical tests20
Back-fitted from spallation — Barre8
Back-fitted from spallation — Westerly13
Shen 2018, from 280 measured spalls10–17

The spallation-inferred modulus is roughly half the mechanically measured one, and that was in print in 1993. So a fit that comes out low is not a defect in our model — it is the documented behaviour of this particular measurement.

The chain, with its hidden assumption exposed

The onset temperature moves with flux because a deeper-soaked rock offers a longer flaw. Writing the stressed volume as a power of the heated depth makes the assumption visible:

V  ∝  ℓqn     ℓq = k (Ts − T0) / q
 
e  =  n / (2m + n)   ⇒   m  =  (n/2) · (1−e)/e

n = 3 — what we use today — assumes the spalling region stays geometrically similar as it grows, i.e. a cube, or a disk of fixed aspect ratio. Note the shape of the result: m is linear in n. Get the geometry power wrong by a factor of two and the modulus is wrong by a factor of two.

The convention is the whole story

At n = 3, with Kant's measured exponent of 0.229–0.306:

m demanded at n = 3, by convention
statistics on…m demandedagainst the literature
flaw length3.40 – 5.05matches nothing published
flaw strength6.80 – 10.10brackets Barre's 8, reaches Shen's 10

The two conventions are related by mstrength = 2 mflaw. Under that substitution the scatter of firing stress across the floor is identical to every digit — so switching conventions buys nothing physical, and it is not a cure for heterogeneity.

But it is decisive for the one external check available to us. It determines whether our fitted modulus sits on a published spallation value or a factor of two below everything in print. With no geometry correction at all, the strength reading lands on Barre granite.

Aspect ratio: a refinement, not a rescue

Real spalls are thin disks, not cubes — and their aspect ratio grows with size, from about 1.5 : 1 at 200 µm to roughly 6 : 1 at 1 mm, with 6.91 recommended from 280 measured spalls. That makes n > 3:

So the aspect ratio tunes us within the literature rather than being needed to get us there. That demotes it from a blocker to a reported sensitivity — which is the right place for it, because a power law fitted from two anchors is not defensible: it predicts 1.21× thickness growth where our own reference note records 1.36×.

The V0 fix, and why the anchor is forced

Separately from the modulus question, one input key is doing two incompatible jobs. Under size_volume = cell, V0 must equal the cell volume or mesh-independence is lost. Under flux, it must be mesh-independent or cross-deck comparison is lost. Those are opposites, so the key has to split.

What has changed is the reason for where the new one is anchored. The size factor multiplies the baseline flaw:

af  =  a0 · ( ℓq³ / Vref )1/m

If the bracket is anything other than 1 at the calibration point, then a0's effective value depends on the fitted m — so the flaw becomes coupled to the modulus we are trying to measure. Factor = 1 is the unique m-independent choice.

That forces the anchor to the leg where the flaw and the relief factor were calibrated — h = 500. At the 850 K target, with the deck's k = 1.5 and Tflame = 1673.15 K:

qnet = 500 × (1673.15 − 850) = 0.412 MW/m²
ℓq = 1.5 × 556.85 / 411575 = 2.029 mm
Vref = ℓq³ = 8.36×10−9 m³

A closed-form constant, because Ts at the anchor is pinned at 850 K by the fit target rather than by R. No circularity, and no new diagnostic.

Anchoring there also leaves the other leg's flaw 2.47× larger at m = 4, which is 1.57× in stress intensity. That spread is the point: it is what pulls the two onsets apart, and today they sit only 2.5 K apart, which is why toughness and relief cannot be told apart at all.

This problem has a name in another field

Cleavage fracture in steels hit it identically. The Beremin local approach integrates stress over a reference volume, and the literature states both things we derived independently: that the reference volume "only scales the microcrack size distribution but does not change the distribution shape," so it and the scale parameter are one calibration; and that taking it as the mesh size is acceptable only "for low stress gradients," where "the mesh size is not significant."

A 2 mm thermal skin carrying 500 K is about as steep a gradient as this field offers. The literature is telling us ours is precisely the case where a mesh-sized reference is not safe.

The founding precedent

Dey & Kranz did this in 1985, "relating heat penetration depths to an assumed Weibull distribution of fracture-nucleation sites." Their four assumptions read as a checklist against ours: spalls form by compressive stress through the same mechanism as uniaxial splitting in a compression test (which is why the relief band is UCS/σ); heat diffuses only a short distance, so the stress state is independent of hole shape; plate buckling governs separation; and Weibull describes strength variation at the spall-thickness scale.

The third is a step we have not built — buckling is named in the founding paper and is our pre-registered next suspect for whatever the relief factor is currently absorbing.

Three things to carry forward

The deck uses the wrong modulus, by the literature's own reckoning

Meier's input_drilling sets m = 20 — Wilkinson & Tester's mechanical value, where spallation calls for 8–13. So the fit is correcting a documented error, not inventing a suspiciously low number.

The load-bearing number is second-hand

The whole reconciliation rests on that 8 / 13 split, and our own reference note states its provenance plainly: three of four primary sources are paywalled and were never fetched, and no number in it came from reading a PDF.

Independent searching corroborates the shape of the story — 10–17 recommended, index 15–20, probable range 15–25 — but not the mechanical-versus-spallation split itself. That paper should be obtained before any of this is written up.

ℓq is a proxy, not a spall

Our anchor's ℓq = 2.03 mm is larger than every measured spall thickness — Wilkinson reports 0.1–1 mm, Höser's 200 µm spalls imply 100–200 µm. The cube over-counts the stressed volume, exactly as the thin-disk geometry predicts. The degeneracy absorbs it, so the fit is unaffected — but ℓq³ should be described as a proxy for the stressed volume, never as the spall's own volume.

And the heterogeneity risk, honestly

Because the scatter is identical under both conventions, the floor's firing stress still varies by 1.8–2.5× at the fitted modulus, against 1.17× at the deck's current m = 20. Half the Meier floor has frozen once before.

But the literature says real granite's spallation modulus is 8–13, and real spallation demonstrably works at that heterogeneity. So nature tolerates this much scatter. The open question is whether our numerics do — which is a narrower and far more answerable question than whether the model is wrong.
