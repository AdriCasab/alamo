The Wall Problem

Nine packets have tried to make the model drill a cylinder instead of a cone. None has succeeded. This is what each one ruled out, why the latest one returned an honest negative, and the four things the next packet has to settle.

What is being built, and what it is scored against

The model is a thermal-spallation drilling simulation: a burner sits in a borehole, heats granite, and the rock flakes off in thin spalls. It is written in ALAMO (an AMReX-based multiphysics code) and is being validated against Meier's 2017 field trial at Grimsel, where a flame-jet burner resting on tripod feet drilled half a metre of granite.

The validation is deliberately narrow. Gas temperature, cuttings absorption and melting all scale every heat flux equally, so they change how fast the model drills but not the shape of the hole it makes. Shape is therefore the only thing that can be scored without circularity — and shape is where the model fails.

To widen a hole, rock at the wall has to be removed. Everything that follows is the story of why it could not be.

Part 1

The cause: a staircase that bleeds heat sideways

The model stores the hole as a height function: for every vertical column of cells it remembers one number, how far down that column has been eaten away. The wall of a drilled hole is therefore a staircase of columns of different heights.

When one column spalls and drops by a cell, its new top cell — which is hot, at the firing temperature — ends up directly beside the buried interior of the taller column next door. That neighbouring rock is still at bulk temperature, and nothing ever heats it. Heat runs sideways across that face and is lost from the drilling process.

The drain

A firing top cell (orange) sits beside cold buried rock in the taller column (blue). Heat flows across that face and never comes back.

voidrock firingcold wall

Why refining the mesh makes it worse

The flux across that face is k·ΔT/dx. Halving the cell size halves dx but leaves ΔT unchanged — so the drain doubles.

2 mm≈ 400 K302 kW/m²58 %

Measured in D2o-0b: the step was 155.1 K at 2 mm and 153.7 K at 1 mm — identical — while the sink it drives doubled. The input flux is ≈ 520 kW/m².

This is not a coding bug. It is a continuum corner singularity: a criterion evaluated at a single point on a geometric corner is mesh-divergent by construction. The practical symptom is that the drilling rate depends on the mesh, which means no number the model produces can be trusted.

The arbiter test, used by every packet since

A stripped-down problem — a static hot disk under a descending exclusion plane, no burner, no jet, no gas model — reproduces the cascade exactly. A packet passes it if the drilling rate measured at 2 mm and at 1 mm agrees to within 5 % in each of three time blocks. That is the whole test: does the answer stop depending on the mesh?

Part 2

Nine attempts, one at a time

What follows is every packet since the mesh problem was found, in order. For each one: what the idea was, what was actually run, what came out, why, and what it left behind for the next attempt.

Two conventions are worth knowing before you read them. First, predictions were written down and hashed before every campaign, made read-only and timestamped before the output directory existed — so no result could be reinterpreted afterwards. Second, every new option had to be off by default and provably inert: with the option off, all 2639 reference output files had to come out bit-for-bit identical. That is what makes each result attributable to the new term and nothing else.

D2ifailed the gate

Is the mesh problem just bad timing?

The idea

When a patch of rock gets hot enough it spalls, and the surface drops by one cell. But the code also made a column wait — an "idle clock" — before it was allowed to fire again. That waiting time was proportional to the cell height. A 2 mm run and a 1 mm run therefore had different waiting times baked into them, which is an obvious way for the answer to end up depending on the mesh.

What was run

Switch the idle clock off completely, then drill the same hole twice — once with 2 mm cells, once with 1 mm cells. If the clock was the cause, the two runs should now agree. The pass mark, fixed in advance: the drilling rate must agree to within 5 % in every 100-second block.

What happened

It failed. Over the matched window the two meshes differed by 13.9 %, and block by block the disagreement grew steadily worse. The count of idle columns was 0 in every block at both meshes, so the clock genuinely was off — this was not a case of the switch not working.

Why

The clock was a real mesh mechanism — removing it delayed the divergence by about 150 seconds. But a second, slower failure sits underneath it and only shows itself once the hole has depth. A further hint came free: setting the clock's threshold to 20 produced byte-identical output to switching it off, which means it was behaving as an on/off threshold rather than as a dial. It was never controlling the thing that mattered.

What it bought: the cause is not timing, and it is something that builds up with depth. That ruled out a whole class of explanations and pointed the next packet at the geometry of the hole rather than at the removal rules.

D2j-0bfound the cause

Take everything away and see whether it still breaks

The idea

The full simulation has a lot going on at once: a burner descending on three feet, a flame jet, a gas energy model, a cone-shaped hole, a rough rock surface. Any of them could be responsible. The way to find out which is to start deleting them.

What was run

A deliberately impoverished problem. A flat block of granite. A uniform hot disk pressed against the top of it. And one horizontal plane descending at a fixed speed which does nothing except switch the heating off above itself — a stand-in for the burner going down. No jet. No feet. No gas model. No burner mechanics of any kind.

Why

Because the mechanism needs nothing but a staircase. The moment one column fires and drops, its hot new top cell is in direct contact with the cold buried interior of the column beside it, and heat pours sideways into rock that is doing no useful work. The flux across that face is k·ΔT/dx: conductivity, times the temperature difference, divided by the cell width.

Now halve the cell size. The temperature difference does not change, because the neighbouring rock is at bulk temperature either way — this was measured directly, 155.1 K at 2 mm and 153.7 K at 1 mm. But dx has halved, so the heat flux doubles. Refining the mesh, which is supposed to make an answer more accurate, makes this one worse.

What it bought: the cause, isolated in a test that runs in minutes. And a rule with real cutting power — if the failure happens under perfectly uniform heating, then no change to the heating pattern can remove it. That one sentence later closed several proposals without running any of them.

D2ktest passedfield run failed

If the sideways face is losing heat, then heat it

The idea

That cold face is cold because nothing in the model heats it. In reality hot gas is flowing past it. So add a heat flux on exposed vertical faces, not only on the horizontal ones the code already heats.

What was run

A new code path, off by default, with the identity gate passed (2639 files unchanged with it off). Then the stripped-down test at both mesh sizes, and separately the full Meier case.

What happened — two results that must not be read as one

On the stripped-down test it worked outright. Mesh disagreement collapsed from −13.5 / −28.7 / −45.5 % to +3.4 / +1.5 / −3.2 %. No stalls, no artefacts, and it did this while drawing only about 7 % of the total face power.

On the Meier case it failed badly. The burner drilled at 4.07 m/h instead of 1.49 — 2.7 times too fast — and both runs drilled clean out of the bottom of the simulation box before the comparison window was even reached.

Why

The implementation gave a vertical wall the same heat transfer coefficient as the floor sitting directly under the flame jet. That is the coefficient for a surface being blasted head-on by an impinging jet, applied to a surface the jet is merely sliding past. Since more absorbed power means more rock removed, the burner simply drilled faster.

What it bought: proof that the mesh divergence is removable — it is not a law of nature. And the exact failure mode that every subsequent packet was written to reject: buying convergence by drilling faster.

D2lstopped — no window

Turn the side heating down until both things are true at once

The idea

Full-strength side heating fixes the mesh problem but drills far too fast. Zero side heating drills at the right rate but leaves the mesh problem. Somewhere in between there ought to be a setting that does both.

What was run

A pure parameter scan — no code change at all. The side heating was scaled by a factor f at 1.0, 0.5, 0.2, 0.1 and 0. Two quantities were defined in advance: f_min, the lowest setting that still suppresses the mesh cascade, and f_rate, the highest setting that still leaves the drilling rate inside Meier's measured 1.3–1.6 m/h band. The rule, also fixed in advance: proceed only if f_rate ≥ f_min.

What happened

f_min came out at 0.5. f_rate came out at 0 — the rate only re-enters the band with the term switched off entirely. The two requirements do not overlap anywhere on the scale. The scan stopped at that point and no code was written.

Why — and this part matters more than the scan

Across the entire ladder the model removed rock at an almost constant 1.32–1.43 joules per cubic millimetre of absorbed energy. So the volume removed is essentially just proportional to the power absorbed, and the whole question reduces to an energy budget.

With the side term completely off, the model already absorbs 6.56 kW — 17.3 % of the burner's 38 kW — and removes 4.58 cm³/s against Meier's 2.47. To reproduce Meier's volume at the model's own conversion efficiency it would need to absorb about 3.46 kW, or 9.1 %. In other words the factor-of-two error is already there with the side term off. D2k's term made an existing error larger; it did not create it.

One honest caveat: the 9.1 % figure rests on an assumed 4–6 J/mm³ energy cost, which is an assumption about how much of the burner's output reaches the rock at all, not a measurement.

What it bought: a whole family of proposals closed at once, and the discovery that the model's apparently-correct drilling rate had been two errors cancelling out.

D2mmixed, and revealing

Is the hole's width set by the flame, or by the tool?

The idea

Meier's burner rests on three feet. The model's burner rests on the highest rock beneath its feet. Perhaps the hole is narrow for a boring reason: the tool is narrow, and the hole is simply the shape the tool carves. If so, the flame physics is not the problem at all.

What was run

Change the burner's foot radius across a 32 mm swing — overall stances of 64, 80, 88 and 96 mm — and measure the minimum diameter of the resulting hole.

What happened

The hole diameter tracked twice the foot radius to within 0.5–2.1 mm at every stance, in both directions. The model's hole is the width of its own tool, almost exactly.

Meanwhile the flame's reach was unaffected by the stance: at 45–50 mm radius the absolute recession rate was the same to within 5–7 % across the whole ladder, while the centre moved by 39 %.

Why this is damning rather than reassuring

If the model's hole is exactly its tool's width and Meier's was wider than his tool, then the agreement is not physics — it is geometry. The rock beyond the footprint has to come off somehow, and nothing in the model can take it off.

A second measurement in the same packet made it worse: the model's floor is not a flat disc with an edge, it is a cone with a long tail. The recession rate is already down to 0.83 of its central value at 29 mm radius and 0.62 at 39 mm, and never falls to a tenth of centre anywhere within 70 mm. Meier's hole is a cylinder.

What it bought: the target is the radial distribution of heat, and the missing capability is removal beyond the footprint — which is the same missing capability the wall needs.

D2o-0jammed the burner

Heat the floor and the wall differently, and see what shape comes out

The idea

Instead of one heat transfer coefficient everywhere, prescribe a high one on the floor, where the jet actually impinges (h = 700), and a low one on the wall, where gas merely flows past (h = 40). Prescribed by hand, not modelled — the point was only to see what shape such a split produces.

What was run

The floor/wall boundary was defined by depth below the nozzle: rock within 45 mm of the nozzle plane counted as "wall", anything deeper counted as "floor".

What happened

The burner jammed. The drilling rate fell from 1.491 to 0.108 m/h, and the hole never even reached 10 cm.

A second, unrelated defect found in the same packet

The prescribed gas temperature was a quadratic curve fitted to data out to 68 mm radius, wrapped in a max(1400, …) guard that existed purely so the input parser could never evaluate a negative number. Applied at large radius, that fit crosses 1400 K at 84.7 mm and goes negative at 198 mm — so beyond 85 mm the safety guard stopped being a guard and became the physics.

What it bought: the boundary must be defined radially, at the foot radius, so the load-carrying columns fall inside the floor zone by construction. Plus two rules with general force — never apply a fit outside the range it was fitted on, and never let a parser-safety clamp set a physical value. Guard with ambient temperature, not with a working gas temperature.

D2o-0bfailed the gate

The same idea, with the boundary at the foot radius

What was run

Identical to D2o-0, except the floor/wall boundary now sits at a fixed radius of 40 mm — the outer edge of the feet — so every load-carrying column is inside the floor zone whatever its height.

What happened

The intended repair worked. Frozen columns fell from 15 to 2, the stall time from 219 s to 11 s, and the drilling rate recovered from 0.108 to 0.771 m/h. But the gate still failed, because 0.771 is about half the control's 1.491.

The reframing that came out of it

This is the packet that changed how the whole problem is posed. The 302 kW/m² draining across that corner is not a loss. It is heat being delivered into the rock immediately behind the wall — which is exactly where the wall needs to get hot in order to spall. The heat is arriving in the right place. The code simply has no way to spend it.

What it bought: the general constraint that any sharp corner in the heating does this, radial or depth-based, so the transition must be smooth over several cells. And the reframing above, which is the standard every later packet is judged against: does the fix turn that drained heat into useful work — without drilling faster?

D2p-0closed before any run

Let the sideways-facing cell fire on its own

The idea

If the wall is getting hot and cannot spall because the spall test is only applied at the top surface, then apply the test to the sideways-facing cell as well.

What happened

The packet was closed during implementation, before a single simulation ran, on three facts each about one search away in the source:

  1. There is no per-cell firing test to reuse. The criterion field is zeroed everywhere and written only at the surface cell. It is inherently a free-surface test that needs a surface and a scan downwards from it.
  2. A side face is heated using its own column's coefficient. So a wall column that is not itself being heated receives no side flux at all, however hot its neighbour is.
  3. Void is derived from a single height per column — so solid sitting above void, in one column, cannot be represented at all.
Two fallbacks checked and rejected the same way

A "slab" rule — levelling any step as soon as it forms — would pass the mesh test trivially, because it removes the steps that the test is measuring, leaving nothing to measure. It is also inert at Meier's 418 K wall, and it removes a whole slab of rock for the cost of heating one cell, which is energetically wrong in a model already removing 1.9× too much. Capping the step height was already answered on the record: a one-cell step has a temperature difference proportional to the cell height, so that particular loss converges on its own.

What it bought: two packets and a design argument refuted in about an hour, for zero compute. And a method rule now applied to every packet: before claiming a mechanism "fits the existing representation", name the variable that holds it and the line that writes it.

D2p-1closed before any run

Smooth the corner over a physical length instead

The idea

D2o-0b showed a sharp corner generates its own drain. So do not make it sharp — spread the transition over a physical length, specifically the distance heat diffuses in the time the burner takes to advance past a point.

What happened

Closed before launch on three counts, all arithmetic.

1. The length had been computed from the wrong material. The project's recorded 3.2 mm came from textbook granite. The model's own inputs give a conductivity of 1.5 and a heat capacity of 2.17×10⁶, which puts the length at 1.604 mm. That correction matters beyond this packet: a 2 mm cell spans 0.8 of the heated layer, not 1.6 of it, so the mesh is coarser relative to the physics than anyone had assumed.

2. The fix points the wrong way. Take D2o-0b's measured 402 K step and spread it over 1.604 mm rather than over one cell. The drain becomes 376 kW/m², or 72 % of the incoming heat — worse than the 302 kW/m² that already halved the burner's rate. And structurally, a smoothing takes heat away from the wall and returns it to the floor, so the floor speeds up, the wall gets colder, and the hole gets narrower — while Meier's hole is wider than his tool precisely because the wall received that heat.

3. The test would have passed by construction. The smoothing length is defined as the larger of the cell size and 1.604 mm. At 2 mm cells the option therefore does nothing at all, and the 1 mm and 0.5 mm runs both clamp to the same 1.604 mm. Two runs sharing the same physical length must agree — so the mesh-convergence test would have returned a clean pass on a model further from the truth than when it started.

What it bought: the rule that generalises past this packet — a criterion that a fix satisfies by construction is not a criterion. Before hashing predictions, check what the proposed fix makes true for free. Also, on the record permanently: corner smoothing is never to be revisited, at any length.

Reading the nine together

Four of them (D2k, D2l, D2o-0, D2o-0b) were attempts to fix the problem by changing how the rock is heated. Every one failed, and D2j-0b explains why in advance: the failure occurs under perfectly uniform heating, so no heating pattern can remove it.

Two of them (D2p-0, D2p-1) were closed by reading the code and doing arithmetic, at zero compute cost — and one of those found the fact that explains all the others.

Part 3

The blocker underneath all of them

D2p-0's third finding is the one that explains the whole sequence. The hole is stored as one number per column. That representation can express a floor going down. It cannot express an undercut — rock removed from the side, with solid rock still above it — because void is defined as "everything above this height".

What the wall actually needs

Heat arrives at the wall at the step's depth. Spalling it means removing a cell with solid rock still above it.

voidfiringneeds to go

What the representation allowed

One height per column. Removing that cell means removing everything above it too — which the physics does not justify.

voidfiringforced to go too

So D2o-0, D2o-0b, D2p-0 and D2p-1 were all testing a physics hypothesis on a data structure that could not host the answer, whatever the answer turned out to be. That is why the next packets stopped arguing about heating laws and went to work on the representation.

Part 4

D2q: building the missing capability, then asking whether it helps

The D2q line was deliberately split into three stages, each with a gate that had to pass before the next began.

Stage 1 (D2q-1) — change the storage, change nothing else

Make the void mask authoritative instead of deriving it from a column height. The single acceptance test was that all 2639 output files stay byte-identical. They did. No physics, no new options, no behaviour change — the capability exists, dormant.

Stage 2 (D2q-2b) — the mechanism

Add an off-by-default option that scores the spall criterion on every exposed face, not only the top one, and removes non-top cells when it fires. Identity passed again with the option off.

The finding that justified the whole staged approach

A deliberately-added interior void was used as a probe, and it caught a bug that had already shipped in Stage 1 while passing byte-identity: incident beam power was being tallied on a "is the cell above me void?" test, so two cells per column qualified and the energy books were out by 14.29 % — predicted and measured to four figures. It is now 2×10⁻¹⁵.

The lesson generalises: byte-identity proves only that nothing changed while the void stayed contiguous. That was demonstrated rather than argued. Two separate bugs in this line have now come from using "the cell above is void" as shorthand for "this is the top of the column".

Stage 3 (D2q-2c) — the arbiter, run 22–23 September

The question: does the drained corner heat now spall the wall, without making the model drill faster? Four runs, two configurations at two mesh sizes, 250 s each.

The side heating was capped at 80 W/m²K. That number was not tuned: it is set just below the coefficient at which a wall would reach its firing temperature within the time it is exposed, derived from the model's own material constants. Raising it to force the mechanism to fire was explicitly forbidden in the packet.

Result

The mechanism never fired. Not once, in any run, with up to ~4000 eligible wall cells present.

This outcome was pre-registered as prediction P4 before the runs, with the instruction that the cap must not be raised to rescue it.

That last point is the most useful thing the packet produced. Going in, the shortfall was believed to be about duration — the wall needed 149 s of continuous exposure and was not getting it. The measurement says otherwise: the wall reaches a steady temperature 80–170 K below firing and stops. It is a steady-state deficit, and that is the number the next packet has to close.

The trap, and how the packet avoided it

Two of the packet's criteria failed: the mesh gap was still −10.9 / −22.2 / −30.3 %, and the rate exceeded its ceiling in 5 of 6 blocks. The available mistake was to read that as evidence against per-cell removal.

It is not, and a baseline registered in advance settles it. An earlier study (D2l's f01) ran side heating at a comparable strength when per-cell removal did not exist in the codebase at all:

A real finding, about something else entirely

Capped side heating largely fixes the cascade when no corner is present: the mesh gap in the control goes from −10.4 / −15.3 / −20.1 % to −3.7 / −4.5 / −2.0 %. With the corner it stays bad. The reason is visible in one number — the corner configuration receives only 35 W of side-face power against the control's 132 W, because the exclusion plane switches the heating off above it.

Which brings the project to the bind it now has to argue its way out of:

Part 5

Two defects that would fire the moment the mechanism starts working

Both were found by inspection during this packet, both confirmed independently in review, and neither affected any number reported — because the mechanism never fired. They matter because they will bite precisely when it does.

Defect 1 — the column-wide label

The code that decides whether a column is "firing" scans the whole column. That was safe only while the criterion was written at the top cell alone. Now a side cell reaching the threshold flags the column — and the top cell spalls, vertically, for the wrong reason.

side cell readywrongly removed

Defect 2 — the moving target

The test for "is this cell exposed sideways?" is run against the live void mask inside the same loop that modifies it. A neighbour removed earlier in the same sweep can expose a cell which then also fires — but only if it is in the same parallel block, since across a block boundary the copy is stale.

Consequence: the claim that removal is independent of how the domain is split across processors is too strong. Narrow, since unexposed cells hold a zero score, but it has to be fixed by testing exposure against a frozen snapshot.

Part 6

What the next packet has to identify

The pre-registered decision rule for an inert result is: report it, and do not raise the cap. The next move is a heating closure that raises the wall temperature honestly. Four things have to be settled, and the first two are prerequisites — without them the first positive result would be an artefact.

Question 1 — prerequisite

Fix the two ordering defects before anything is made hotter

The column-wide label and the live-mask exposure test both produce wrong removals only once wall cells start reaching the threshold. They must be repaired, and the repair gated on the same byte-identity sweep, before any packet raises the wall temperature.

Question 2 — prerequisite

Is "inert" physical, or a coarse-mesh artefact?

The wall runs ~100 K hotter at 1 mm than at 2 mm. The trend runs towards firing as the mesh refines, so the verdict "unreachable under this cap" is established at two mesh sizes and contradicted in direction by both of them. One 0.5 mm point settles it. Cheap, and it decides whether the negative result stands.

Question 3 — the substance

Can the wall be heated to 822 K by a defensible physical argument?

This is the real question and it is now quantified: the wall needs +76 K at 1 mm, +172 K at 2 mm, as a steady-state gain, not a longer exposure. The physical case for it already exists in the field record and has never been applied in the model — Meier's burner is a body inside the hole, an 80 mm skirt and a water-cooled shield inside an 85–95 mm bore, which implies confined impingement of 700–1500 W/m²K where the model currently applies grazing flow at ~40.

The corresponding trap is equally clear, and D2k already demonstrated it: heating strong enough to suppress the cascade doubles the absorbed power and makes the burner drill 2.7× too fast. So the closure has to raise the wall temperature without raising total absorbed power — which means redistributing heat, not adding it.

Question 4 — owed, and cheap

How much drain is still being lost?

The diagnostic k·ΔT/dx at the transition, measured against D2o-0b's 302 kW/m² / 58 % of input, was requested regardless of verdict and is missing from the write-up. It is the direct measure of how much of the corner heat is still going nowhere, and it is recoverable from the existing output files without re-running anything.

What the D2q line has and has not established

It has established that the code can now represent and remove an undercut, that under a physically-derived cap an exposed wall never reaches its firing point in 250 s, and that the deficit is steady-state rather than dwell-limited.

It has not established that the mechanism is wrong, untestable, or unable to spall a wall that is hot enough. That remains untested, and Questions 1 and 2 must be answered before it is tested.

Method

Why nine failures is not nine wasted weeks

Each packet in this sequence is a closed experiment with its predictions written down and hashed before any run, read-only, timestamped before the output directory exists. That discipline is what makes the negatives usable:

  • Two packets were closed for zero compute cost (D2p-0, D2p-1) — one by reading three lines of code, one by arithmetic that showed the proposed fix was worse than the problem and would have passed its own test by construction.
  • Byte-identity gates — every source change in the D2q line had to leave all 2639 reference output files bit-for-bit unchanged with the new option off. That is what makes each campaign's result attributable to the new term alone.
  • Attribution baselines registered in advance — without D2l's earlier scan sitting on disk, D2q-2c's failed criteria would have looked like a verdict on the new mechanism instead of a verdict on side heating.
  • Pre-registered negative branches — "the mechanism stays inert" was written down as an outcome, with the instruction not to raise the cap to escape it, before the runs started. That is why this result is reportable rather than arguable.
  • Anchors chosen before the fact — one of D2q-2c's criteria fails against the anchor it was registered against and would pass against a different, equally arguable one. Both are recorded; the pre-registered one governs.

The net position after nine packets: the cause is identified and reproduced in a minimal test, the representation that blocked every fix has been replaced, the remaining gap is a single measured number (76–172 K of wall temperature), and the physical argument that might supply it is already in the field record, unexploited.
