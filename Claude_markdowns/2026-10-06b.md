# Granite properties for the Meier / Kant model — literature review and temperature-dependent laws (2026-10-06)

Written as a reference for the planner and implementer. Every number carries its source; where a value is an
estimate assembled from mineral data rather than a rock measurement it is marked **[estimate]**. Model inputs
today (`input_drilling`, `CTRL4_FIXED/FH/metadata`): ρ = 2750, c_p = 790 const, κ_th = 1.5 W/mK const
(α_diff = 6.9e-7), E = 30 GPa (per brief §9), Weibull a0_ig = 8 µm, m = 20, T_0 = 293.15 K. θ denotes °C,
T kelvin. The rock is Central Aare granite from the Grimsel area: 46 % quartz, 31 % K-feldspar, 20 %
plagioclase, 3 % biotite, grain sizes up to ~4 mm (Kant et al. 2017a, samples from Chüenzentennlen, 1 km
north of the Grimsel Test Site).

## 1. Specific heat capacity

**Primary source.** Kant, Ammann, Rossi, Madonna, Höser & Rudolf von Rohr, *Thermal properties of Central Aare
granite for temperatures up to 500 °C: irreversible changes due to thermal crack formation*, Geophys. Res. Lett.
44, 2017 (doi 10.1002/2016GL070990; open access via the ETH research collection). Laser-flash (Netzsch LFA 467,
ASTM E1461) on three 25 mm discs, 25–500 °C in 25 K steps, three heating/cooling cycles. Room temperature:
c_p = 0.768 ± 0.048 kJ/(kg·K), λ = 3.247 ± 0.066 W/(m·K), κ = 1.656 ± 0.09 mm²/s, ρ = 2550 kg/m³ (five
bulk measurements). Their Table 2 fits (all data within a 10 % band):

    c_p(θ) = 1.37 − 178/(θ + 271)   kJ/(kg·K)        (same on every cycle — no hysteresis)
    λ₁(θ)  = 2000/(θ + 563)         W/(m·K)          (first heating, intact rock)
    λ₂₊(θ) = 3400/(θ + 1385)        W/(m·K)          (after one excursion to 500 °C)
    κ₁(θ)  = 487/(θ + 264)          mm²/s;   κ₂₊ = 526/(θ + 400)

**Cross-checks.** (i) Whittington, Hofmeister & Nabelek 2009 (Nature 458:319, erratum 458:... "10⁻⁶ should
be 10⁶"): for a crustal assemblage of 30 % quartz, 60 % albite, 5 % phlogopite, 5 % annite (molar mass
221.78 g/mol), C_P = 199.50 + 0.0857 T − 5.0×10⁶ T⁻² J/(mol·K) for T ≤ 846 K and 229.32 + 0.0323 T −
47.9×10⁶ T⁻² above. Divided by 0.22178 kg/mol this is 750 J/(kg·K) at 293 K and 1161 at 773 K — within
2 % of the Kant law over 300–550 °C (Table 1). (ii) The granite law already in the repo's Zhang/Oglesby
reference, c_p = −2.73×10⁻⁴ θ² + 0.894 θ + 788, agrees to 3 %. (iii) Eibenstock granite 0.706 → 0.912
J/(g·K) from 25 to 600 °C (Acta Geotechnica 2020). (iv) Vosteen & Schellschmidt 2003, Fig. 4: mean magmatic
c_p rises from ~0.75 at 0 °C to ~1.0 kJ/(kg·K) at 300 °C.

**Recommendation.** Use the Kant law as written up to 773 K (its measured range) and continue it to 873 K;
the extrapolation to 823 K differs from Whittington by 3 %. Above the α–β quartz transition (846 K)
Whittington's second branch drops to ~880 J/(kg·K) because β-quartz has a lower c_p; the λ-peak itself
(a few kJ/kg over ±20 K) is not in either law. For this programme (T_s ≈ 760–880 K) that matters only if
T_s is pushed above ~850 K. The enthalpy integral has a closed form and should be the quantity the model
stores: H(θ) = 1370 (θ − 20) − 178 000 ln((θ + 271)/291) J/kg from a 20 °C datum; the inversion θ(H) is
monotone and smooth, so the existing bisection works unchanged.

**What it changes.** Mean c_p from 20 °C to the firing temperature is 1005 J/(kg·K) at 760 K and 1021 at
823 K, against 790 today: the enthalpy per volume of removed rock rises from 1.01 to 1.24 GJ/m³ at the
present firing temperature (ρ 2650 in place of 2750 included), i.e. the energy-limited rate falls by
0.81×, more if T_s rises (§7).

## 2. Thermal conductivity and diffusivity

**Primary source.** Kant 2017a, same measurements (Table 2 above). Two curves matter: the intact-rock law
λ₁ for rock that is being heated for the first time (everything below the flake layer), and the cracked-rock
law λ₂₊ for rock that has already been to ~500 °C (the top millimetre, where thermal microcracks along grain
boundaries raised the crack density from 1.6 to 5.8 mm/mm² and cut λ by 25 % at room temperature; the two
curves converge above ~450 °C). c_p shows no such hysteresis.

**General law.** Vosteen & Schellschmidt 2003 (Phys. Chem. Earth 28:499), Eq. 4–5, for magmatic and
metamorphic rocks 0–500 °C: λ(T) = λ(0)/(0.99 + T (a − b/λ(0))) with a = 0.0030 ± 0.0015, b = 0.0042 ±
0.0006, T in °C, and λ(0) = 0.53 λ(25) + ½√(1.13 λ(25)² − 0.42 λ(25)). Seeded with Kant's 3.25 W/(m·K)
it reproduces the intact Kant curve within 5 % everywhere (Table 2), so it is a safe form for any other
granite whose room-temperature value is known. The repo's Zhang law κ = 1/(0.3154 + 3.8×10⁻⁴ θ) is the same
shape within 10 %. Eibenstock granite: 3.65 → 1.8 W/(m·K) from 25 to 600 °C.

**What the model has.** κ_th = 1.5 W/(m·K) and α_diff = 6.9×10⁻⁷ m²/s, both constant. On the intact curve
1.5 W/(m·K) is the value at about 1060 °C — nothing in the heated layer is that hot. At room temperature the
rock conducts 3.2–3.4 W/(m·K) and diffuses at 1.66 mm²/s, 2.4× the model's value; at the firing temperature
(≈490 °C) the measured values are 1.9 W/(m·K) and 0.64 mm²/s. So the present constants are roughly right at
the surface and 2× low in the body. The steady ablation rate does not depend on λ, but the thermal-layer
thickness κ/v, the surface gradient (hence the gradient-aware criterion and the flake thickness ℓ), the
start-up transient and the wall heating all do; expect the flake layer to become thicker and the gradient
shallower when λ(T) goes in.

**Recommendation.** λ(θ) = 2000/(θ + 563) as the single law (intact); carry λ₂₊ as a sensitivity on the
surface band. With c_p(θ) and ρ fixed, κ follows and must not be set independently.

## 3. Density

Central Aar granite 2660 ± 24 kg/m³ and Grimsel granodiorite 2706 ± 14 (Krietsch et al. 2018, Sci. Data,
from Keusen et al. 1989); Kant's thermal samples 2550; Kant & von Rohr 2016 Gotthard granite 2594, Bethel
2566. The model's 2750 is at the top of the range; **2650** is the defensible central value. The volume
change to 500 °C is ≈1 % (Kant 2017a) and can be ignored, or taken from α in §4.

## 4. Thermal expansion **[estimate]**

No measured α(θ) for Central Aare granite was found today (Kant 2017b JGR, which uses one, is behind the
Wiley wall). The number is assembled from the minerals, which is legitimate here because the rock's α is
dominated by its quartz and the quartz data are excellent:

- α-quartz at 20 °C: 13.55×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ (a-axis) and 7.43×10⁻⁶ (c-axis) (recommended values; arXiv
  1612.07049 §III citing Bechmann & Kosinski / Sosman); temperature polynomials referenced to 0 °C,
  a₁₁ = 13.71 + 6.5×10⁻³ θ − 1.9×10⁻⁶ θ², a₃₃ = 7.48 + 2.9×10⁻³ θ − 1.5×10⁻⁶ θ² (×10⁻⁶ /°C), valid to
  ~300 °C. Toward the α–β transition at 573 °C the expansion diverges: the a and c axes extend by roughly
  1.5 % and 0.9 % between 25 °C and the transition (Kihara 1990; Carpenter et al. 1998 — **recalled, not
  re-read today**), i.e. an isotropic mean α of ≈24×10⁻⁶ over the range and ≈35×10⁻⁶ between 300 °C and
  the transition; Carpenter et al. report a further 0.63 % volume jump at the transition itself.
- Feldspars: 5–8×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹, weakly temperature dependent (Fei 1995 AGU Reference Shelf); taken here as
  5.5 + 3×10⁻³ θ (×10⁻⁶).
- Rock-level checks: granites 5.5–8×10⁻⁶ at room temperature (Britannica 8 ± 3; Eibenstock granite 6–8);
  Nanan granite (only 12 % quartz) rises by 62 % at 400 °C, 95 % at 500 °C and 175 % at 600 °C relative to
  200 °C (Acta Geod. Geophys. 2022); dilatometry on several granites gives 35–43×10⁻⁶ averaged over
  25–1000 °C (J. Therm. Anal. Calorim. 2015); every source attributes the rise above ~300 °C to quartz.

Volume-fraction mixing with 46 % quartz gives Table 3's α_rock: 8.4×10⁻⁶ at 20 °C (matching the ~8×10⁻⁶
used by the ETH group), 9.5 at 300, 16 at 400, 23 at 500, 27 at 550 °C. The uncertainty above 300 °C is
±30 %; below it ±10 %. Two cautions. First, the differential expansion that drives spallation is the same
one that cracks the grain boundaries (Kant 2017a §5), so part of the high-temperature α is accommodated by
microcracks and does not load the flake as thermal stress — a reason to keep the bulk α at the low side
near T_s. Second, the model's criterion uses α only through σ = Eα ΔT/(1−ν); what matters is the product
E·α, see §5.

## 5. Elastic modulus and Poisson's ratio

**Room temperature.** Krietsch et al. 2018 (Grimsel Test Site dataset, from Keusen et al. 1989): Central
Aar granite E = 53.3 ± 11.0 GPa, ν = 0.37 ± 0.12, UCS 169 ± 37 MPa; Grimsel granodiorite 47.3 ± 15.4 GPa,
ν 0.33 ± 0.15, UCS 117 ± 48; dynamic moduli 65–81 GPa at 30 MPa confinement. Kant & von Rohr 2016: Gotthard
granite 54 GPa, Bethel 58. The brief records E = 30 GPa in `input_drilling`; that is a 1.8× under-estimate
of the static value and should be corrected together with any refit of a0 (σ ∝ E).

**Temperature dependence.** Measured *at* temperature: Mount Yan granite E = 29.98 − 0.0145 θ GPa to
400 °C (−19 %), ν = 0.1185 + 4×10⁻⁴ θ (ScientificWorldJournal 2014, PMC3980840); the Geofluids 2022
real-time series (25–700 °C) finds E and UCS rising slightly to 200–300 °C, then falling, with the
deterioration clearly set in above 400 °C. Measured after cooling (which overstates the loss because the
cracks stay open): Eibenstock granite 62 → 60 → 45 → 20 GPa at 25/400/600/800 °C; coarse granites lose
~60 % by 600 °C. Heuzé 1983 (Int. J. Rock Mech. 20:3, the standard review) gives the same picture: little
change to ~300 °C, then a steepening decline through the quartz transition.

**Recommendation.** E(θ)/E₀ = 1.0 to 200 °C, 0.85 at 400, 0.65 at 500, 0.5 at 600 °C (Table 3), ν = 0.25–
0.30 and rising slowly. Note the product E·α (Table 3, last column): flat to 300 °C, then 1.7–1.8× at
400–550 °C. The thermal stress for a given ΔT is therefore ~1.8× the constant-property value at the present
firing temperature, which — unless the flaw size is refitted — lowers T_s. This is the "wrong way" effect
noted on 29 September, now with numbers.

## 6. Fracture toughness and tensile strength

**Room temperature.** Westerly granite 1.79 ± 0.02 MPa·m½ (Meredith & Atkinson 1985, PEPI 39:33); Lanhelin
granite 1.52 (Chandler, Meredith, Brantut & Crawford 2017, GSL Spec. Publ.); Fujian granite 1.31 (Geothermal
Energy 2022, 10:...); heat-treatment baselines 1.2–1.7 (RSOS 2019; Sci. Rep. 2025). Central Aar granite
specifically: the Stanford 2018 summary gives 1–1.5 (not verified against the PDF text); Dutler et al. 2018
report anisotropic Grimsel values of order 1.2–2.3 (**recalled**). Use **1.5 ± 0.3**. Tensile strength:
Central Aar 9.06 ± 1.48 MPa, Grimsel granodiorite 6–11 (Krietsch 2018).

**Temperature dependence, measured at temperature** (the relevant kind): Chandler 2017, Lanhelin granite
pre-treated to 150 °C so only the temperature effect remains: 1.52 (22 °C), 1.65 (54), 1.58 (86), 1.49
(118), 1.39 (150), 1.37 (175), 1.31 MPa·m½ (200 °C) — a small rise then −14 % by 200 °C. Geothermal Energy
2022, Fujian granite at 0.001 mm/min: 1.31 (25), 1.10 (100), 1.03 (200), 1.01 (300), 0.92 (400), 0.99
(500 °C), fit K_Ic = 1.051 + 0.489 e^(−θ/86.4): −25 % by 400 °C, flat after. Meredith & Atkinson 1985
(Westerly, 20–400 °C, at temperature): initial increase then gradual decrease, attributed to crack blunting
then crack coalescence (values not retrieved today). After cooling the losses are much larger (1.21 → 0.81
at 400 °C → 0.32 at 600 °C, RSOS 2019; 1.74 → 0.59 at 400 °C, Sci. Rep. 2025) — those are damage curves and
do not apply to rock that is still hot and uncracked ahead of the front.

**Recommendation.** K_Ic(θ)/K_Ic,0 = 1.0 to 100 °C, 0.90 at 200, 0.82 at 300, 0.78 at 400, 0.75 at 500 °C,
dropping toward 0.6 at the quartz transition (Table 3), ±0.1.

## 7. Spallation temperature

**Measurements.**
- Wilkinson & Tester 1993 (Rock Mech. Rock Eng. 26:29), pyrometry under flame jets, from the thesis figures
  (brief §9): Barre granite 480 / 560 / 680 / 830 / 930 °C at 0.3 / 0.6 / 1.2 / 2.0 / 2.9 MW/m²; Westerly
  450 / 600 / 650 / 740 °C at 0.3 / 1.2 / 1.5 / 2.9 MW/m². They concluded that mechanically determined Weibull
  parameters do not describe spallation; the slope of T_s against q fits m ≈ 8 (Barre) and 13 (Westerly).
- Kant & von Rohr 2016 (repo `validation/kant`), Gotthard and Bethel granite under a methane–air burner:
  sensor-plate flux 0.45–0.87 MW/m², h_fl 630–1010 W/(m²·K), flame 1100–1450 °C; mean spalling surface
  temperature ≈ 600 °C, failure-plane ≈ 550 °C, minimum ≈ 500 ± 50 °C, ≈100 °C drop on each detachment,
  minimum h ≈ 500 W/(m²·K); Gotthard stops spalling between SOD 130 and 140 mm (q ≈ 0.5–0.6 MW/m²).
- Kant et al. 2017b (JGR 122:1805): 550–600 °C for granitic rocks; spall thickness Δx = 200 µm (Höser 2016).
- Rossi 2018 (Stanford workshop summary, unverified): spallation initiated at ~400–600 °C surface temperature.

**Law.** Weibull flaw statistics give ΔT_s ∝ q^(3/(m+3)) (Rauenzahn 1986 Eq. 4.91): a thinner heated layer
stresses a smaller volume and needs a higher stress to find a flaw. Fitting Wilkinson's points gives an
exponent of 0.31 for Barre (m = 6.8) and 0.23 for Westerly (m = 10.1). Kant's Gotthard points (ΔT ≈ 440–
560 K at 0.45–0.87 MW/m²) lie on or slightly above the Westerly line. The repo's m = 20 gives an exponent of
0.13, i.e. an almost flux-independent T_s.

**Recommendation.** T_s(q) = 293 + 530 (q / 0.6 MW m⁻²)^0.25 K, ±50 K (Table 4): 800 K at Meier's ≈0.5
MW/m², 823 K at 0.6, 895 K at 1 MW/m². The model's 757–767 K is 40–60 K low at Meier's flux and would be
130 K low at 1 MW/m². The exponent is the more important correction: it is what makes the model's rate
respond less than linearly to flux, as the experiments do. In the criterion this is the Weibull modulus
(m ≈ 8–12 for the T_s(q) slope, not the mechanical 20) together with the flaw size; both should be fitted
jointly to the Kant legs (0.45–0.87 MW/m²) and checked at Meier's 0.5, with E, α, K_Ic from §4–§6 in place
first — otherwise a0 absorbs the property errors.

## 8. Flake thickness (for completeness)

Höser 2016 (ETH): mean spall ≈ 200 µm on Central Aare granite, used by Kant 2017b as Δx; Rossi et al. 2020
(J. Pet. Sci. Eng. 190:107005, field test): populations at 250 µm and 1 mm; Meier: p90 < 300 µm; Wilkinson:
0.4 mm at 6×10⁻⁴ m/s; Rauenzahn: Weibull median 2.67 ℓ/m. The model's floor flakes (0.2 mm) are right; the
wall flakes are capped at 1.5 mm and are not.
