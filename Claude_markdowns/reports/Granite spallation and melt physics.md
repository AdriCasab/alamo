# Buckling, not a reference depth, sets spallation's floor

Research report, 2026-11-02. Synthesised from five research notes in `Claude_markdowns/research_notes/Granite spallation and melt physics/` (onset and properties; minimum flux and flakes; microwave absorption; beam-regime observations; hot-spot thermoelastic stress). Labels are preserved from the notes: **MEASURED** = an experiment on the named rock in the cited paper; **MODELLED** = output of a model; **ESTIMATE** / **INFERRED** / **COMPUTED HERE** = assembled or derived by a note-writer; **[fig]** = read off a published figure; **[via X]** = taken from a secondary source. "Hot" = tested at temperature; "cooled" = heated, cooled, then tested.

The literature answers the eleven questions with one consistent picture, and it is not the one the model encodes. Every dataset that resolves the *first* spall on a granite puts the onset in a flat **430–630 °C band across 0.2–7.4 MW/m²**, falling slightly, not rising, toward the threshold, and the 93 %-quartz Sioux quartzite spalls lowest of all at **~355 °C**, so the quartz α–β inversion at 573 °C does not pin the onset and was formally rejected on petrographic grounds in 1964 and 1989 ([Rauenzahn & Tester 1989, on disk](/Users/tzetze20/amr_tools/alamo/franck_material/RockFailureMechanism.pdf); [Wilkinson & Tester 1993](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01019868)). The only *derived* minimum heat flux in the literature is Preston's buckling condition made quantitative by Dey & Kranz: a flake can buckle only if a flaw spans roughly ten times the heated-layer thickness δ ≈ kΔT_s/q, so a bounded flaw population switches spallation *off* below q_min while the onset temperature stays flat above it, a step rather than the **879 K → 1001 K climb** the model's 2 mm constraint depth produces between 1.6 and 0.34 MW/m² ([Walsh, Lomov & Roberts 2011](https://gdr.openei.org/files/174/WalshLomovRoberts_GRC_2011.pdf)). Half-space thermoelasticity has no reference depth at all: the in-plane stress is −EαΔT(z)/(1−ν) pointwise, a sub-surface temperature peak leaves the cooler surface in *reduced compression*, never tension, and the "cold skin in tension" of microwave-heated cylinders is a finite-body term that a borehole floor does not have ([Lu & Fleck 1998](https://www.fleckmech.org/papers/96.pdf)). On the electromagnetic side, granite's loss tangent at 2.45 GHz rises only ×2 by 500 °C and ×5.6 by 700 °C with **no signature of the quartz transition** ([Hartlieb/MPN dataset, Zenodo 4835664](https://zenodo.org/records/4835664)); the one sharp absorption event anyone has measured is the melt itself, which nucleates 2–4 mm below the surface, cuts off transmission, and raises the heating rate fifteen-fold ([Woskov & Michael 2011](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/11ja/11ja024/11ja024_full.pdf)). No measured granite permittivity exists at 90–140 GHz at any temperature, so the model's 300 m⁻¹ is an undocumented warm-rock value; no one has ever reported spallation of granite under millimetre waves, so the user's ~10 GHz spall-then-melt observation is a new regime point. Of the six hypotheses, H3 (onset temperature plus a separate buckling-type thickness condition) and H6 (melt becomes more absorptive) are supported; H1, H2, H4 and H5 are excluded on the evidence below.

## Granites first spall in a flat 430–630 °C band that quartz's inversion does not set

Six granites, four laboratories and three heating methods agree on a material band, not a phase temperature. Rauenzahn & Tester's propane-torch series on Barre and Westerly granite gives onset rises of **465/435/445/472 K** and **473/446/423/455 K** above ambient at 0.68–1.19 MW/m², with "quite small, if any" flux dependence, and the authors state that "none of the temperatures calculated from the propane experiments were above 573 °C, even with maximum levels of uncertainty included" (MEASURED, 2000 fps video plus calorimetry; [R&T 1989, pp. 392–397, on disk](/Users/tzetze20/amr_tools/alamo/franck_material/RockFailureMechanism.pdf)). Wilkinson & Tester's IR-scanner campaign put the *global* onset for Barre and Westerly at **400–600 °C over 0.2–2.9 MW/m²**, "relatively insensitive to the applied heat flux level", while the **93 %-quartz Sioux quartzite spalled at 331–364 °C** at every flux from 0.71 to 2.29 MW/m² with onset and steady-state temperatures identical (MEASURED; [W&T 1993, Table 5 and Fig. 15](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01019868)). Kant & Rudolf von Rohr resolved individual spalls with a 1.5 mm, 1 kHz pyrometer on Gotthard and Bethel White granite and found θ_SP ≈ 600 °C before and θ_FP ≈ 550 °C after each spall, with no clear standoff trend across 0.45–0.87 MW/m² (MEASURED [fig]; [Kant & von Rohr 2016, on disk](/Users/tzetze20/amr_tools/alamo/franck_material/MinimalBoundaryConditions.pdf)). On the project's own rock, Höser recorded **430–600 °C** one IR frame before the first spall left Grimsel granodiorite at 4.3–7.4 MW/m², "any trend was not recorded" with firing rate (MEASURED [fig], ±25–45 °C; [Höser 2016 notes](/Users/tzetze20/amr_tools/alamo/tests/MMWSpalling/validation/hoeser/hoeser_2016_thesis_notes.md)), and Kant et al. measured a failure-plane temperature of **553–694 °C (mean ≈ 630 ± 40 °C)** on Central Aare granite from the same quarry, falling to ≈ 560 ± 35 °C under 48 MPa uniaxial load, against their own LEFM prediction of 475 (390–560) °C (MEASURED [fig] vs MODELLED; [Kant et al. 2017 JGR, on disk](/Users/tzetze20/amr_tools/alamo/franck_material/JGR%20Solid%20Earth%20-%202017%20-%20Kant%20-%20A%20theory%20on%20thermal%20spalling%20of%20rocks%20with%20a%20focus%20on%20thermal%20spallation%20drilling.pdf)).

The quartz-transition explanation has a traceable history and a traceable refutation. It was a Soviet thermal-drilling hypothesis (Vasiliev 1958, cited as ref. 28 by Rauenzahn & Tester); the Canadian Mines Branch found "no petrographic evidence for the α–β transition in quartz at 573 °C" in 1964 and concluded those transformations "are certainly of little or no consequence"; Thirumalai's 1969 Bureau of Mines study found spalling in quartzite "at temperatures considerably below the high-low inversion of quartz"; and Rauenzahn & Tester added their own sub-573 °C data as "more evidence against such a claim" ([R&T 1989, pp. 385 and 397](/Users/tzetze20/amr_tools/alamo/franck_material/RockFailureMechanism.pdf); [Thirumalai 1969, OSTI 5804838](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/5804838)). Quartz *does* govern spallability, but through its 4.98 × 10⁻⁵/°C volumetric expansion against ≈ 2 × 10⁻⁵/°C for feldspar, which the inclusion model turns into ~10 MPa of tension around a quartz grain for a 40 °C rise near a free surface, and through stiffness and the absence of a soft phase ([R&T 1989 Eqs. 6–8, Fig. 7](/Users/tzetze20/amr_tools/alamo/franck_material/RockFailureMechanism.pdf)). A transition-pinned onset would place the quartz-richest rock *at or above* 573 °C; the data put it ~200 °C below. The single genuine 573 °C signal in the literature is Glover et al.'s acoustic-emission peak during slow furnace heating, which is bulk microcracking in a uniformly heated sample over minutes, not a surface onset (MEASURED; [Glover et al. 1995](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1365-246X.1995.tb01852.x)).

The one rising curve in the literature is a different quantity. Wilkinson & Tester's *steady-state* spalling-surface mean climbs from ≈ 500 to 930 °C on Barre between 0.6 and 2.9 MW/m² because partly buckled flakes overheat before ejection, an effect absent in Sioux quartzite because it has no soft phase ([W&T 1993, Tables 3–5 and §8](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01019868)). Kant & von Rohr's Fig. 14 claim that "the spallation temperature increases linearly with heat flux" is built by appending those steady-state means on other rocks, taken by another instrument, above 1 MW/m² to their own 0.45–0.87 MW/m² onset points, so the regression mixes two definitions ([Kant & vR 2016, §3.4](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijrmms.2015.12.009)). The two series that actually walk down to the threshold drift slightly *downward*: Gotthard 620 → 570 °C and Bethel 630 → 540 °C as standoff grows from 50 to 130–150 mm [fig], and Barre under the laser from 630 to 423 °C within scatter ([Kant & vR 2016 Fig. 11](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijrmms.2015.12.009); [W&T 1993 Table 5](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01019868)). The model's +120 K climb over 1.6 → 0.34 MW/m² lies outside every measured band's scatter (Höser ±50 °C, Kant ±40–50 °C), and its 843.76 K = 570.6 °C firing temperature sits inside Höser's band only by numerical coincidence: it is where the *estimated* α-tripling between 300 and 500 °C drives σ = E∫α dθ/(1−ν) to the flaw criterion.

Exposure times matter for how "no spall" is judged. Onset is a few-seconds event in every laboratory: ≤ 2 s for the torch, < 0.5–5 s for Höser's jet, "within a few seconds" to ≈ 600 °C for Kant, with thermal penetration √(κt) of 0.8–2.8 mm at 0.5–5 s ([R&T 1989 p. 393](https://doi.org/10.1016/0148-9062(89)90935-2); [Höser et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijrmms.2018.04.007)). The no-spall durations at Kant's 140–160 mm standoffs are not stated (traces run to ≥ 90 s), and Meier's hydrothermal flame shows that a 150–250 mm block saturates in ~10 min, after which "the state of stress in the rock probe starts to decrease as the temperature profile flattens" and cavity growth stops (MEASURED; [Meier et al. WGC 2015](https://pangea.stanford.edu/ERE/db/WGC/papers/WGC/2015/21040.pdf)). A simulation that declares no spall at 0.12 MW/m² must therefore state its exposure time and body size.

## No Grimsel expansion curve exists, and hot-tested stiffness loses only a quarter by 400 °C

The project's α(θ) table is explicitly an estimate, and nothing in the literature replaces it. Kant et al.'s GRL paper on Central Aare granite measured diffusivity, heat capacity and conductivity to 500 °C over three cycles (diffusivity and conductivity fell to ~75 % after the first cycle) but no thermal expansion (MEASURED, no α; [Kant et al. 2017 GRL](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2016gl070990)), and the project reference itself says "No measured α(θ) for Central Aare granite was obtained ... assembled from the minerals", tabulating 8.4 → 28.4 × 10⁻⁶/K from 20 to 650 °C with ±30 % above 300 °C (ESTIMATE; [project reference §4](/Users/tzetze20/amr_tools/alamo/tests/MMWSpalling/validation/grimsel_properties/grimsel_granite_properties_reference.md)). The hot-measured analogues support the shape but not the plateau: Westerly and Stripa granite rise from **6–12 to 10–15 × 10⁻⁶/K between 40 and 325 °C** under 6–55 MPa confinement, with confinement lowering α by 1–5 × 10⁻⁶ because microcracks control the response (MEASURED; [Heard & Page 1982](https://doi.org/10.1029/JB087iB11p09340)); Climax quartz monzonite rises from 8–11 to **22–25 × 10⁻⁶/K at 475 °C** (MEASURED; Page & Heard 1981, companion to the JGR paper); eight Egyptian granites with 24.5–37.7 % quartz show 11–15 × 10⁻⁶/K to 300 °C and "the first sharp increase ... around 570 °C" by dilatometer and DSC (MEASURED; [Alzahrani et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8950072/)); Eibenstock granite shows steep increases at both 573 °C and 870 °C (MEASURED; [Wang et al. 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11440-020-00926-8)). Real dilatometer curves therefore *spike* at ≈ 570 °C and fall back to a lower β-quartz value before a second step near 870 °C; the project table's flat 28 × 10⁻⁶/K from 600 to 650 °C is an artefact of stopping the quartz ramp at the inversion. A further caution: part of the measured rock α above 300 °C is crack opening (Heard & Page; P-wave velocity −50 % at 450 °C in Westerly, [Griffiths et al. 2018](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2017JB015191)), which relieves rather than loads a confined skin, so a stress model should use the pressure-confined values or cap α as the project reference recommends.

The quartz content of the rock itself carries a ±20 % compositional uncertainty. The ETH drilling group reports Central Aare granite from the Chüenzentennlen quarry at **46 % quartz, 31 % K-feldspar, 20 % plagioclase, 3 % biotite** (MEASURED; [Kant et al. 2017 GRL via project reference](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2016gl070990)), while XRD on Grimsel Test Site granodiorite cores gives **~32 % quartz, 38 % plagioclase, 20 % K-feldspar, 8 % biotite** (MEASURED; [Wenning et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.5194/se-9-683-2018)). Both Höser's "Grimsel granite/granodiorite" and Kant's "Central Aare granite" come from the same quarry, so `Grimsel.H` is that rock; room-temperature mechanical values compiled from Keusen are E 53.3 ± 11.0 GPa, ν 0.37 ± 0.12, UCS 169 ± 37 MPa, tensile 9.06 ± 1.48 MPa for Central Aare granite (MEASURED; [Krietsch et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/sdata.2018.269)).

High-temperature strength data divide sharply by protocol, and the division matters for the model. Hot-tested granite loses modestly and gradually: Mount Yan granite's E falls linearly from 30.0 to 23.6 GPa (−19 %) by 400 °C (MEASURED; [Wang et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3980840/)); Fujian granite's real-time K_Ic falls from ≈ 2.46 to ≈ 1.23 MPa·m⁰·⁵ between 25 and 500 °C at the fastest loading rate but only to 76 % at the slowest (MEASURED; [Yang et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40517-022-00225-3)); under 130 MPa confinement Westerly granite's peak stress drops only from **815 MPa at 600 °C to 719 MPa at 900 °C** and the rock stays brittle to 900 °C, becoming ductile only at 1000 °C (MEASURED; [Violay et al. 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-017-08108-5)), consistent with Wong's 95-experiment finding that dry Westerly stays brittle to 700 °C with temperature "a stabilizing factor" (MEASURED; [Wong 1982](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0167663682900205)). Heard & Page's unconfined hot E fell by up to a factor of 2 by 350 °C, but raising pressure from 6 to 55 MPa raised E 3–6×, so the loss is crack-controlled and largely removed by confinement ([Heard & Page 1982](https://doi.org/10.1029/JB087iB11p09340)). Cooled specimens collapse instead: Eibenstock E ≈ 62/60/45/20 GPa at 25/400/600/800 °C ([Wang et al. 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11440-020-00926-8)); Pingyi granite K_Ic 1.21 → 0.81 → 0.32 → 0.20 MPa·m⁰·⁵ at 25/400/600/800 °C with a "brittle–ductile transition" identified at 500–600 °C and attributed to crack density, not the quartz transition (MEASURED; [Niu et al. 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-98787-2)); granite tensile strength 10.59 → 2.84 MPa by 600 °C ([Sci. Rep. 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-59470-0)). These cooled curves measure accumulated crack damage that the material ahead of a spallation front has not experienced. The project's E/E₀ = 0.5 and K_Ic/K_Ic,0 = 0.6 at 600 °C sit between the two families; extrapolating the hot linear trends gives ≈ 70–75 % of E and 50–65 % of K_Ic at 570 °C, higher than the model's values, but no hot dataset with tabulated values above 500 °C was reachable (Zhang 2022 to 700 °C and Shao 2015 to 800 °C exist behind refused pages), so the plateau above 600 °C has no measured basis either way.

One conflict with the project reference must be flagged here: the note read Yang et al. 2022 as K_Ic ≈ 2.46 → 1.23 MPa·m⁰·⁵ (25 → 500 °C), whereas the project reference records the same paper as 1.31 → 0.99. The two readings disagree in absolute level; the ratio (~0.5–0.76 depending on loading rate) is the safer quantity to carry.

## The only derived minimum flux is a buckling bound that predicts a step, not a climb

Four families of onset theory exist, and only one contains a minimum flux. Gray's 1965 criterion compares the biaxial compression of a laterally confined half-space to compressive strength, ΔT_s = (1−ν)σ_c/(βE), and finds ΔT_s "essentially constant" across the thirteen Mines Bureau rocks, a pure temperature criterion with no flux dependence and no floor (MODELLED; [R&T 1989 p. 385](https://doi.org/10.1016/0148-9062(89)90935-2)). The Russian school kept Gray's ΔT_s and added a layer that "must support the entire linearized temperature difference", δ_s = k_r ΔT_s/q, which gives a thickness ∝ 1/q but still lets the layer thicken without bound as q → 0 (MODELLED; same source). The Weibull-volume models from Dey 1984 through Rauenzahn & Tester, Wilkinson & Tester and Walsh & Lomov give a *steady* spalling temperature ΔT_s ∝ Q^{3/(m+3)} (exponent 0.13 at m = 20), so the onset gets slightly *easier* as flux falls and nothing stops it: Rauenzahn & Tester applied Eq. 14 to a 2 m gneiss exhaust passage spalling at 0.006 m/h under 300 °C gas and obtained a spallation temperature of 150 °C, a model happily spalling at kW/m² fluxes (MODELLED vs field; [R&T 1989 pp. 390–392](https://doi.org/10.1016/0148-9062(89)90935-2)). A full-text search of both MIT theses for "minimum flux", "threshold" and "cease" found no floor; the only cut-off there is an *upper* one, where the rock becomes plastic ([inhole heat notes §5](/Users/tzetze20/amr_tools/alamo/tests/MMWSpalling/validation/hoeser/rauenzahn_wilkinson_inhole_heat_notes.md)). Kant et al.'s LEFM criterion Sp = K_I/K_Ic ≥ 1 for a 20 µm edge crack under σ = −EαΘ/(1−ν) is flat in flux, lowered by confinement, and again contains no minimum even though the same group measured one a year earlier (MODELLED; [Kant et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/2016JB013800)); their SGW 2017 drilling-velocity model is continuous to h → 0 with only a melting cap ([Kant et al. SGW 2017](https://pangea.stanford.edu/ERE/pdf/IGAstandard/SGW/2017/Kant2.pdf)).

The one derived minimum is geometric. Preston's mechanism, adopted as Figure 1 of every LLNL report, has a flaw grow parallel to the surface under in-plane compression until "the high aspect ratio (diameter to thickness) of the resulting plate and the high compressive stress cause buckling" ([W&T 1993 §4](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01019868)). Rauenzahn & Tester's Eqs. 15–16 give the Euler plate-strip condition: the chip buckles when its cracked length divided by thickness exceeds π/√(6(1+ν)β_r ΔT_s) ≈ 10 (MODELLED; [R&T 1989 p. 390](https://doi.org/10.1016/0148-9062(89)90935-2)). "Building on Preston's mechanism of spall production, Dey and Kranz [1985, 1987] employed plate-buckling theory to **bound the heat flux required** to generate spalls from a granite surface ... relating heat penetration depths to an assumed Weibull distribution of fracture-nucleation sites", and the LLNL group adds that "the traditional buckling model of spall-production is essentially a bifurcation event" ([Walsh, Lomov & Roberts 2011](https://gdr.openei.org/files/174/WalshLomovRoberts_GRC_2011.pdf); [Walsh & Lomov 2013 LLNL-TR-632239, on disk](/Users/tzetze20/amr_tools/alamo/franck_material/Walsh_2013_ModelingThermallyInducedFailure_LLNL-TR-632239.pdf)). The logic is two separate inequalities: a fixed stress (fixed ΔT_s) criterion for the flaw to grow, and a geometric condition d_flaw ≥ C_L·δ for the layer to buckle. Because δ ≈ kΔT_s/q thickens as flux falls, a bounded largest flaw d_max implies q_min ≈ C_L·k·ΔT_s/d_max; the note's own arithmetic with k = 3.2 W/mK and ΔT_s ≈ 550 K gives **q_min ≈ 1.2 / 0.9 / 0.45 MW/m² for d_max = 15 / 20 / 40 mm** (COMPUTED HERE, not Dey & Kranz's published number, whose full text was unobtainable). Those are exactly the observed 10–20-grain spall diameters and the measured Kant minimum. The prediction is a flat onset temperature above q_min and *no* spall at all below it, a step. Only two measured minima exist: Kant & von Rohr's Gotthard granite spalled at standoffs of 50–130 mm and not at 140–160 mm, Bethel White at 50–150 and not at 160, with stagnation-point sensor fluxes of **720/866/755/609/450 kW/m²** and h = 766/1009/873/747/633 W/m²K at 50–150 mm, summarised as "θ_SP,min = 500 °C and h_fl,min = 500 W/(m²K)" (MEASURED; [Kant & vR 2016, Tables 1 and 4](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijrmms.2015.12.009)); and the empirical "~1 MW/m² and ~500 °C" rule traced to Rauenzahn, quoted by Meier ([Meier et al. WGC 2015 §3.2](https://pangea.stanford.edu/ERE/db/WGC/papers/WGC/2015/21040.pdf)) and as "~450 °C and ~0.5 MW/m²" by Beentjes. The MIT laser data spalled Westerly at 0.23 MW/m² (457 °C) and Barre at 0.31 MW/m² (481 °C), so the true threshold on those rocks lies below those values ([W&T 1993 Tables 3–5](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01019868)). Höser's lowest Grimsel flux of 2.5 MW/m² still removed 7 cm³/min, so no Grimsel minimum exists ([Höser 2016 Fig. 5.25](https://doi.org/10.3929/ethz-a-010825044)).

The model's constraint depth is structurally the Dmitriyev layer frozen at 2 mm. With k = 3.2 W/mK and ΔT = 550 K, kΔT/q equals 2 mm at q ≈ 0.9 MW/m², so the model reproduces a q_min of the right order; but by taking the *driving* ΔT across a fixed 2 mm instead of fixing the stress by the surface rise, it converts the geometric cut-off into a temperature penalty, and the onset climbs. None of the four published families predicts a climb: buckling gives a step, Weibull gives a gentle fall, LEFM is flat, and the heat-loss/finite-body arguments of Browning, Meier and Beentjes give a *time*-dependent cut-off when the body saturates ([Beentjes et al. 2018 §1, §5](https://pangea.stanford.edu/ERE/pdf/IGAstandard/SGW/2018/Beentjes.pdf)). Walsh & Lomov's related finding, that inserting a fixed physical length (the ~100–400 µm aspect-ratio transition) into the Weibull model changes the predicted surface temperature "significantly" when the scaled thermal penetration approaches it, confirms the general point that any fixed length put into a volume criterion manufactures flux dependence ([Walsh & Lomov 2013 §4](/Users/tzetze20/amr_tools/alamo/franck_material/Walsh_2013_ModelingThermallyInducedFailure_LLNL-TR-632239.pdf)).

Flake thickness data are scarce, old, and all point the same way. The Canadian Mines Bureau's field average was **0.4 mm, "less than a grain"**; laboratory torch spalls were "lamellar, typically 10–20 grains in diameter and perhaps one to two grains thick" with aspect ratios 8–15 for Geller's chips and ≈ 10 from buckling (MEASURED, qualitative; [R&T 1989 pp. 389–393](https://doi.org/10.1016/0148-9062(89)90935-2)). The LLNL shadow-method survey of Potter Drilling field spalls found aspect ratios of ≈ 6:1 above 5 mm, 4:1 near 1 mm, and 2:1–1:1 at 200 µm, with the collapse at ~400 µm "related to the average grain size", thicknesses up to ~2 mm (MEASURED; [Walsh & Lomov 2013, Figs. 11–12](/Users/tzetze20/amr_tools/alamo/franck_material/Walsh_2013_ModelingThermallyInducedFailure_LLNL-TR-632239.pdf)); Hu et al. confirmed size-dependent aspect ratios on 280 granite spalls and recommend 6.91 (MEASURED; [Hu et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.petrol.2018.08.050)). Höser's "200–300 µm" for Grimsel is a laser-diffraction *fragment* peak of the < 500 µm sieve fraction, not a thickness, and Kant's SGW 2017 Δx = 200 µm is set equal to it by assumption ([Höser 2016 Figs. 5.27–5.30](https://doi.org/10.3929/ethz-a-010825044); [Kant SGW 2017 §2.2](https://pangea.stanford.edu/ERE/pdf/IGAstandard/SGW/2017/Kant2.pdf)). The only thickness theories are the Weibull spall-size distribution (median ξ₀ = 2.9 in m·V·x/κ, giving x_med ≈ 0.10 mm at 6 m/h; MODELLED, [R&T 1989 Eqs. 17–24](https://doi.org/10.1016/0148-9062(89)90935-2)) and the layer rule δ = kΔT_s/q, both ∝ 1/q with no cap and differing by ~20× in absolute value. The note's arithmetic places measured flakes between the two rules: the layer rule gives 0.24–0.41 mm at Höser's 4.3–7.4 MW/m² against her 200–300 µm fragments, and 1.8 mm at 1 MW/m². The model's h = 0.4 × skin depth sits within the measured band at ≥ 1 MW/m², but its **1.5 mm cap** equals kΔT_s/q at q ≈ 1.2 MW/m², so below that flux the model's flake is thinner than either rule predicts and the cap acts as an undeclared minimum-flux rule. Walsh's aspect-ratio collapse says flake thickness has a *floor* near the grain size, not the ceiling the model imposes. ## Half-space thermoelasticity has no reference depth and leaves a cooled surface in compression

The stress law for a traction-free half-space whose temperature varies only with depth is local. Taking Lu & Fleck's free-plate result σ_xx(z) = σ_yy(z) = Eα[T̄ − T(z)]/(1−ν), where T̄ is the thickness mean, and letting the plate thickness go to infinity with a heated layer of finite depth, the mean term vanishes and **σ_xx(z) = σ_yy(z) = −EαΔT(z)/(1−ν), σ_zz = 0, pointwise in z** ([Lu & Fleck 1998](https://www.fleckmech.org/papers/96.pdf)). No reference depth appears: the stress at any depth is set by the local temperature rise over the far field because the body is infinitely deep (zero membrane and bending resultants) and laterally infinite (zero in-plane strain). The model's rule σ ∝ [ε_th(surface) − ε_th(d_ref)] reproduces this only when T(d_ref) = T₀; whenever T(2 mm) is above ambient, which is exactly the low-flux, thick-thermal-layer case, it under-predicts compression and can invert the sign while the true local stress is still compressive. The INFERRED consequence for the millimetre-wave case is sharper still: with a Beer–Lambert deposition depth of 3.3 mm (α = 300 m⁻¹) exceeding the 2 mm reference, T(d_ref) tracks T_s closely and the model's drive nearly vanishes, which would predict *less* spallation under volumetric heating than under a flame at equal surface temperature, the opposite of what the user observes at ~10 GHz. The textbook pages (Boley & Weiner, Timoshenko & Goodier, Nowacki, Parkus) were not reachable, so the result is reconstructed from the open plate formula and from Welsh et al.'s reference to the "uniform two-dimensional compressive stress" of laterally uniform heating ([Welsh, Tuchman & Herman 1988](http://www.columbia.edu/~iph1/Download/40.%20Welsh-Thermal%20Stresses%20and%20Strains%20Induced%20in%20Laser%20Processing%20-Journal%20of%20Applied%20Physics%2064,%206274%20(1988)%20doi.org.10.1063.1.342086.pdf)).

A localised hot spot reduces the compression but does not change its sign. Sternberg & McDowell (1957) and Muki (1956) proved that for any steady surface temperature distribution on a half-space "the stress field is plane and parallel to the boundary", σ_zz = σ_rz = σ_θz = 0 everywhere ([Muki 1956](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/kikai1938/22/123/22_123_795/_article/-char/en); [Sternberg & McDowell 1957, abstract](https://pubs.ams.org/journals/qam/1957-14-04/S0033-569X-1957-87367-8)). Welsh et al.'s full solution for a steady Gaussian beam gives the axis stress as **σ_rr = σ_θθ = −EαT_max/2**, i.e. (1−ν)/2 ≈ 0.37 of the 1-D scale S = EαΔT/(1−ν) for ν = 0.25, with σ_zz and σ_rz two orders of magnitude smaller and peaking half a spot radius down ([Welsh et al. 1988, eq. 26](https://pubs.aip.org/aip/jap/article-abstract/64/11/6274/15663/)). The note's derivation from the planar-stress theorem reproduces this exactly and extends it: σ_rr = −(Eα/2)·T̄(r) (compressive everywhere) and σ_θθ = −EαΔT + (Eα/2)·T̄, which is tensile only where the surface temperature falls faster than 1/r. For the true steady profile under a Gaussian flux the hoop stress stays compressive at every radius; for a transient Gaussian *temperature* a hoop ring of **+0.109 EαT₀ at r = 1.79a** appears; for a hard-edged top-hat it reaches +EαΔT/2 just outside the spot (COMPUTED HERE, validated at the axis against Welsh). This matches the millisecond-laser silicon result in which "hoop stresses ... are compressive within the laser spot and convert to tensile stress outside the laser spot" with radial cracks initiating at the hoop maximum ([Appl. Opt. 2011](https://opg.optica.org/ao/abstract.cfm?uri=ao-50-21-3725)). The ratio of thermal penetration to spot radius, δ/a, is what interpolates between the 1-D state (thin skin under a wide spot: full S, laterally confined by its own cold substrate) and the steady compact spot (≈ 0.37 S).

Volumetric heating with a sub-surface temperature peak does not put the surface skin in tension in this geometry. The local law holds for any T(z), monotonic or not: a surface cooler than the peak but hotter than ambient carries −EαΔT_s/(1−ν), reduced compression, with the compression maximum at the temperature peak. The familiar "internally compressed and externally tensioned" state of microwave-heated basalt cylinders, with interior temperatures exceeding the surface by > 150 °C ([Energy 2023, abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360544223006199); [Hartlieb et al. 2012](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892687512000271)), and Hasselman's "thermal trapping" stresses in semi-absorbing plates ([Hasselman 1966](https://ceramics.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1151-2916.1966.tb13218.x)), are *finite-body* effects: for a free slab with a parabolic internal profile, Lu & Fleck's formula gives a surface tension of +(2/3)Eα(T_c − T_s)/(1−ν) that is proportional to the mean-temperature term a half-space lacks. In three dimensions the steady theorem gives σ_rr + σ_θθ = −EαΔT(r,z) at every depth, so the surface trace above the peak is negative and tension would require a surface profile narrower than its own mean. Surface tension over a volumetric spot therefore needs one of three things none of which is the Beer–Lambert half-space: active cooling of the surface below ambient (Yaseen's cooling phase, where "the failure took place in the subsurface areas in which the tensile stresses overtake the tensile strength", [Yaseen et al. 2014](https://pangea.stanford.edu/ERE/pdf/IGAstandard/SGW/2014/Yaseen.pdf)), grain-scale mismatch between absorbing and transparent minerals (Toifl's Voronoi simulations show "high maximum principal stresses on the boundaries of the microwave absorbing phase", MODELLED, [Toifl et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mineng.2016.01.001)), or a finite body. The one open analysis of volumetric laser absorption in a half-space (Tayel 2020) was abstract-only.

The fracture mode follows from the stress state. A compressive hot skin under a wide spot produces Preston spallation: surface-parallel flaw growth, a buckling flake of thickness ~δ, and a mosaic of flakes narrower than the spot, with "compressive stress in the surface layer and tensile stresses beneath it", spallation compressive stress at **29–51 % of UCS**, and "the size of the buckling layer ... smaller than the size of the spallation zone leading to a mosaic pattern" (MODELLED on shale; [Guo et al. 2024, abstract](https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/publications/mechanics-of-thermal-spallation-and-fracturing-of-dry-rocks-produ/)). Grain-scale GEODYN simulations add that fractures start at grain boundaries and asperities and "run along the thermal front", and that flat surfaces "withstand substantially higher heat fluxes before spalling", so classical half-space buckling may *over*-estimate the required stress (MODELLED; [Walsh, Lomov & Roberts 2011](https://gdr.openei.org/files/174/WalshLomovRoberts_GRC_2011.pdf)). A steady compact spot needs ≈ 2.7× the surface ΔT to reach the same stress and so favours melting first, which is the laser experience: "firing the laser beam at the same spot for too long ... melted the rock and increased the specific energy" ([US 7,416,258 B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7416258B2/en)). The transient hoop ring makes radial surface cracks that remove nothing. For the MMW configuration (spot ~cm, absorption depth 3–10 mm, surface cooled) the geometry sits between the two limits with the compression peak sub-surface; the INFERRED mode is still delamination, nucleating at or below the temperature peak with a thicker flake of order the absorption depth. No source treats this configuration directly.

## Granite's microwave loss rises gently, and the melt, not a runaway, is the sharp step

Only one temperature-resolved permittivity dataset for granite exists, and it is at 0.4–3 GHz. Hutcheon and Mouris (Microwave Properties North) measured a single granite rod by cavity perturbation from room temperature to 1000 °C in 25 °C steps, the data behind Hartlieb et al. 2016 and the Toifl simulations ([Zenodo 4835664](https://zenodo.org/records/4835664); [Hartlieb et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mineng.2015.11.008), abstract only). At 2.47 GHz tan δ is **0.0069 at 22 °C, flat to ~300 °C, ×2.0 at 500 °C, ×3.15 at 600 °C, ×5.6 at 700 °C**, dips between 750 and 900 °C as an irreversible mineral transformation starting at ~675 °C lowers both ε′ and ε″, and reaches ×8.9 at 995 °C (MEASURED); the corresponding power penetration depth 1/α shrinks from 1.2 m to 0.38 m at 600 °C and 0.14 m at 995 °C (COMPUTED HERE). The dataset documentation is explicit: "The data shows no evidence of the influence on either ε′ or ε″ of the well-known 571 °C 'network puckering' ... transition in quartz" (MEASURED, verbatim). After the run the rod had bleached from grey to white and its room-temperature loss was **9× lower than virgin rock** (tan δ 0.0008), implying that part of the virgin loss is an adsorbed or hydrous phase driven off irreversibly (INFERRED). Across 0.9–3 GHz, ε″ falls as f^−0.41 at every temperature (COMPUTED HERE), the signature of conduction-like, thermally activated loss; feldspar alkali-ion conduction with ~1 eV activation energy is the natural carrier ([Hu et al. 2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00710-014-0325-7)). Pure quartz is a very weak absorber even at 1000 °C (ε″ ~10⁻³–10⁻²; [Microwave Properties North](https://microwavepropertiesnorth.ca/glasses_insulation_materials/)), and heating-rate rankings in cavities consistently place biotite and hornblende as the heaters, feldspars intermediate, quartz poorest ([Zheng et al. 2020, abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S136516092030887X)).

At millimetre wavelengths the record is one room-temperature number. Woskov & Michael measured Barre-type granite at 28 GHz by slab transmission: index 2.24 and a *field* attenuation of 0.14 Np/cm, "corresponding to a 1/e propagation depth of 7.1 cm" (MEASURED; [Woskov & Michael 2011, PSFC/JA-11-24](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/11ja/11ja024/11ja024_full.pdf)). The *power* coefficient a Beer–Lambert source uses is twice that, 28 m⁻¹, giving a 3.6 cm power 1/e depth and tan δ ≈ 0.0213 (COMPUTED HERE), roughly 3× Hartlieb's room-temperature granite; extrapolating Hartlieb's own f^−0.41 law to 28 GHz gives ε″ ≈ 0.014 against Woskov's 0.107, a 7× discrepancy between two granites and two methods. No measured granite permittivity at 90–140 GHz, hot or cold, was found: the 137 GHz numbers in the MIT and Quaise literature are *melt emissivities* (0.64 ± 0.05 for black glass), not solid absorption coefficients, and the DOE final report states the group's own model outright: "before the rock melts it is assumed to be near the measured room temperature value and after melting it is increased about two orders of magnitude to restrict absorption mainly to the surface" (MODELLED; [DE-EE0005504 final report](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1169951), [mirror PDF](https://www.ing-goebel.de/app/download/5812950994/Electro-Magnetic_Wave_Drilling_MIT_Experiment.pdf)). Scaling Woskov's anchor to 137 GHz with constant tan δ gives α ≈ 137 m⁻¹ (d_p 7.3 mm) at room temperature and, applying Hartlieb's temperature ratio, ≈ 275 m⁻¹ at 500 °C and 431 m⁻¹ at 600 °C (INFERRED). The model's **α = 300 m⁻¹ requires tan δ ≈ 0.045**, the ≈ 500 °C row on the Woskov anchor or the ≈ 750 °C row on the Hartlieb anchor: a plausible warm-rock value that no source measured.

The temperature steepening is weaker, not stronger, at higher frequency. A conduction loss contributes ε″ = σ/(2πfε₀), falling as 1/f, so if the 450–750 °C rise in granite is conductive the *fractional* steepening with temperature is ≈ 4× weaker at 10 GHz and ≈ 56× weaker at 137 GHz than the Hartlieb ratios in the table (COMPUTED HERE); the 137 GHz column is an upper bound on self-sharpening and the true hot-solid depth there is probably nearer the room-temperature 7 mm than 1–2 mm. This is consistent with Woskov's transmission trace, which stayed finite until the melt step. For the user's ~10 GHz rig the inferred room-temperature penetration depth in dry Barre-like granite is ~10 cm, shrinking to perhaps 3–5 cm at 600–700 °C; at that frequency granite is a volumetrically heated body.

Thermal runaway is documented, modelled and exploited, but only in rocks whose conductivity climbs steeply toward melting. Jerby's microwave drill works because "a positive temperature dependence of the dielectric losses ... accelerates the temperature growth-rate and the thermal-runaway process", with the simulated growth rate taking off "above ∼600 °C" in alumina, concrete and basalt ([Jerby et al. 2002, Science](https://www.eng.tau.ac.il/~jerby/microwave_drill/Microwave_drill.pdf)); for basalt the fitted law is σ(T) = 110·exp(−12093/T) S/m below 1360 K, a molten core forms at 1400 K after 70 s at ≤ 1 kW, and lava erupts with a dusty plasma above 1500 K (MEASURED + MODELLED; [Jerby & Shoshani 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6736850/)). The note's evaluation of that σ(T) shows the conduction loss only becomes comparable with basalt's fixed dipolar loss within ~200 K of melting (COMPUTED HERE). Granite behaves differently in every reachable experiment: surface heating ≈ **1 °C/s at 0.9 kW** in a multimode cavity ([Hassani et al. 2016, snippet](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/286903630_The_influence_of_microwave_irradiation_on_rocks_for_microwave-assisted_underground_excavation)); 250–440 °C after 60 s at 3.2 kW ([Hartlieb et al. 2018](https://pure.unileoben.ac.at/en/publications/reaction-of-different-rock-types-to-low-power-32-kw-microwave-irr/)); a melt cavity "near the biotite-rich area at 600 °C" and complete melting at 800 °C in 20 mm cubes at 1.4 kW, with feldspar and biotite, not quartz, melted by XRPD, and UCS falling from 88 to 19 MPa (MEASURED; [Zeng et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00710-019-00681-z)); 670.5 °C with "cracking and melting" at 6 kW for 360 s ([Kahraman et al. 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1365160919302771)); and at 24 kW through an open waveguide a 50 cm granite block reached only ≈ 150 °C with an extensive crack network and no melt ([Hartlieb & Grafe 2017](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00501-016-0569-0.pdf)). No author reports a solid-state runaway in granite, and a Petroleum Science review's claim that granite's melt "is composed mainly of quartz" conflicts with Zeng's XRPD and with quartz's measured low loss ([Petroleum Science 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1995822622000024)).

The one abrupt absorption event common to all granite experiments is the melt. At 28 GHz, virgin granite heated at **7.4 ± 0.2 °C/s** with 1.3 kW absorbed over an equivalent 82 cm³ volume, melt onset came at 1235 ± 45 °C corrected against the published 1215–1260 °C solidus, and the second heating of the already-glassed surface ran at **110 ± 5 °C/s**, "more than an order of magnitude faster", because the black glass absorbed at the surface (MEASURED; [Woskov & Michael 2011](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/11ja/11ja024/11ja024_full.pdf)). The DOE report adds that "the melt initially starts 2–4 mm below the surface and breaks through to the surface from below", that 28 GHz transmission through a 2.5 cm slab "is rapidly cut off when the 137 GHz thermal emission jumps", and that surface reflection steps from 0.14 before melting to 0.3 after ([DE-EE0005504](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1169951)). The race in a granite floor is therefore not skin-sharpening against melting but volumetric heating until a sub-surface melt nucleates, then a switch to surface-limited absorption. Spalling under microwaves is reported for basalt ("violent thermal fracture ... fragments would spall from the top surface" at 28 GHz with 0.3–0.9 kW/cm²; "surface spalling, bursting or melting" at 2.45 GHz) and never explicitly for granite, which cracks, bleaches over a ~30 mm weakened halo, and loses strength instead ([DE-EE0005504](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1169951); [Lu et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00603-019-1743-y)).

## Millimetre waves have only ever melted granite; spallation lives in the laser and flame record

The regime map assembled from twenty-one observations has a clear structure once absorption depth and exposure time are added to intensity. Every millimetre-wave test on granite sits at **10⁶–2 × 10⁷ W/m² on 2–5 cm spots for 30 s to tens of minutes**, and every one melted. Oglesby's granite slab under 800 W on a 2 cm waist (peak ≈ 1.3 × 10⁶ W/m², derived) reached **1410 K at 50 s and 1716 K at 150 s**, above the 1488–1533 K melting range, then 2400–2870 K at 2.3–3.2 kW without reaching the 3233–3503 K vaporisation band (MEASURED, digitised ±100–200 K; [Zhang et al. 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0266352X23003282); [local digitised table](/Users/tzetze20/amr_tools/alamo/tests/MMWSpalling/validation/zhang/zhang_oglesby_mmw_validation_summary.txt)). Woskov's 20 mm TE11 launcher at ~2 kW melted granite and basalt "within 2–3 minutes", 4.5 kW raised melt temperatures to 2500–3000 °C in 5–10 minutes, and "the very high temperature gradient (> 400 °C/cm) ... caused significant thermal fracturing" that forced steel banding of every sample (MEASURED; [Woskov 2017, PSFC/JA-17-8](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/17ja/17ja008/17ja008_full.pdf)). At 45 GHz, 5.5 × 10⁶ W/m² for 30 s gave 969 °C and no melt while 1.65 × 10⁷ W/m² for 60 s gave 2033 °C and melt (MEASURED; [Liu et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10762-022-00855-5)); together with Oglesby's point this brackets the MMW melt onset near a fluence of order 10⁸ J/m² (INFERRED). The closest MMW observation to spall-like disintegration is the 8 mm of "loose sand-like rubble" that came off a granite borehole wall at the melt rim, a side effect, not a removal mechanism ([Woskov & Oglesby 2016, PSFC/RR-16-5](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/16rr/16rr005/16rr005_full.pdf)). MIT's patent assigns millimetre waves to melting and vaporisation and mentions spallation only as short pulses to fracture a glassy wall ([US 8,393,410 B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8393410B2/en)); Quaise names its target regime "full bore vaporization" ([Houde et al. 2021](https://publications.mygeoenergynow.org/grc/1034509.pdf)).

Quantitative spallation regimes exist only for lasers and flames. The GTI/ANL/CSM programme found that "short illumination by a single laser beam of irradiance about 1000 to 5000 W/cm²" (1–5 × 10⁷ W/m²) spalls rock, that shale at 4217 W/cm² spalled at 0.5 s (0.52 kJ/cm³) and melted at 1.0 s (3.6 kJ/cm³), and that "the mechanism changed between spalling and melting by controlling either the specific power or exposure time" (MEASURED, mostly sedimentary rocks; [US 7,416,258 B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7416258B2/en)); Xu et al. place the minimum specific energy "in the spalling zone just prior to the onset of mineral melt" ([Xu, Reed & Graves 2004, abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/328361783_Laser_spallation_of_rocks_for_oil_well_drilling)). For granite specifically the quoted laser boundaries are a spall onset of 230 W/cm² (2.3 × 10⁶ W/m²) and an "estimated" spall-to-melt transition at 5200 W/cm² (5.2 × 10⁷ W/m²), but their provenance is an abstract and must be treated as weak ([Research Square rs-7725822](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-7725822/v1)). A 26–27 kW fibre laser on a 50 mm spot for 30 s produced granite spalls 0.2–1 mm across and ~0.5 mm thick at ~550 °C (MEASURED; [uptake-gap notes](/Users/tzetze20/amr_tools/alamo/tests/MMWSpalling/validation/hoeser/literature_uptake_gap_notes.md)). The laser spallation window overlaps the MMW intensity range; the discriminator is exposure time and absorption depth. A surface-absorbing wavelength with sub-second dwell builds a steep gradient in a thin skin; at 28 GHz absorption depths of centimetres the surface reaches the solidus before a spall-depth layer is stressed (INFERRED). The user's ~10 GHz observation of spallation at lower intensity and melting at higher has no published counterpart; the nearest brackets are the weakly provenanced laser figures above and the flame-jet record, which stops below 10⁷ W/m².

Once a millimetre-wave beam melts granite, the melt pools and becomes the absorber. "Basalt melts flowed much better than granite melts"; granite melt "did not flow to the walls" and filled only the central 4.3 cm of a 5 cm borehole; a 12 mm leak hole was drilled so melt could leave (MEASURED; [PSFC/JA-17-8](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/17ja/17ja008/17ja008_full.pdf); [PSFC/RR-16-5](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/16rr/16rr005/16rr005_full.pdf)). Melted walls were ~4 mm thick after 41 min at 4.9 kW, "an indication of beam wall absorption depth". The 28 GHz emissivity of granite melt, measured calorimetrically and equal to the absorbed fraction, is **0.66 ± 0.03, rising to 0.70 ± 0.03 at vaporisation at 2710 ± 120 °C**, 0.91 inside a borehole with power trapping, and "larger than in the infrared" ([Woskov & Michael 2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10762-011-9851-0); [PSFC/RR-16-5](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/16rr/16rr005/16rr005_full.pdf)). The melt temperature is capped by radiation, "about 0.4 kW/cm² at ∼3000 °C" (4 × 10⁶ W/m²), so that a 60 % power increase raised surface temperature only 17 %, and by air-plasma breakdown that "generally limited [power] to less than 4 kW" on a 32 mm beam at atmospheric pressure, with radiometer spikes marking breakdown events and an air purge of up to 235 lpm needed to suppress them ([PSFC/JA-17-8](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/17ja/17ja008/17ja008_full.pdf); [DE-EE0005504](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1169951)). Removal happens only through a leak path or vapour pressure: face pressures "far exceed the injected purge pressure (5x increase for low-power)" and the melt is "pushed into the walls of the hole before vitrifying into a glass-like lining" that then thermally cracks ([Houde et al. 2021](https://publications.mygeoenergynow.org/grc/1034509.pdf); [New Atlas 2025, press](https://newatlas.com/energy/quaise-energy-millimeter-wave-drill-demo-houston/)). Laser glaze behaves oppositely: fused rock "reflects or absorbs successively irradiated laser beams ... causing the rock destruction process to cease completely" ([Laser destruction and treatment of rocks, snippet](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/253916443_Laser_destruction_and_treatment_of_rocks)), because at optical wavelengths the glass is a dielectric mirror while at millimetre wavelengths the melt's ionic conductivity collapses the skin depth. Oglesby's 2700–2900 K plateau is the radiation cap, not vaporisation, so a vapour-front rule should not fire below ~3.2–3.5 × 10⁶ W/m² net absorbed flux unless the hole traps radiation (INFERRED). Heat budget anchors: 12.5 kJ/cm³ to take granite to 3000 °C including 0.9 kJ/cm³ of fusion, a melt threshold of ~4.5 kJ/cm³, ~14 kJ/cm³ to vaporise ([PSFC/JA-17-8](https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/17ja/17ja008/17ja008_full.pdf)).

## Conclusion

The literature replaces one mechanism with two. The model's constraint depth tries to make a single quantity, the temperature difference across 2 mm, do the work of both a stress criterion and a thickness criterion, and in doing so manufactures the one feature no dataset shows: an onset that climbs toward the threshold. The published picture is a fixed-stress onset set by local surface temperature and material properties, flat with flux within ±50 °C, plus a separate geometric condition that compares the heated-layer thickness kΔT_s/q with the largest flaw a flake can span; the second condition switches spallation off below roughly 0.5–1 MW/m² for centimetre-scale flaw populations without touching the onset temperature above it. The same half-space thermoelasticity that removes the reference depth also removes the hope that volumetric heating puts the surface in tension; what volumetric deposition does is move the compression maximum and the likely delamination plane below the surface and thicken the flake toward the absorption depth, which is a prediction the user's rig can test directly.

Two numbers in the model now carry no documented support and one carries the wrong physics. The absorption coefficient of 300 m⁻¹ is a warm-rock value at 137 GHz that no one has measured, and its temperature dependence, if conductive, is tens of times weaker at millimetre wavelengths than the 2.45 GHz curve suggests; the 1.5 mm flake cap acts as a hidden minimum-flux rule; and the α(θ) table's plateau above 600 °C is an artefact. The sharp event worth encoding is the solidus, where absorption steps up by orders of magnitude, reflection rises from 0.14 to 0.3, and heating accelerates fifteen-fold, with the melt nucleating a few millimetres down. That the user sees granite spall at ~10 GHz where the millimetre-wave record sees only melt makes the ~10 GHz experiments the only place the spall-to-melt boundary for a volumetrically heated granite can be measured.

## Verdicts on the six hypotheses

| Hypothesis | Verdict | Deciding evidence |
|---|---|---|
| H1 Quartz-transition-pinned onset | **EXCLUDED** | First-spall band 430–630 °C flat over 0.2–7.4 MW/m² on six granites; 93 %-quartz Sioux quartzite spalls at ~355 °C; Barre/Westerly onsets below 573 °C "even with maximum levels of uncertainty"; refuted petrographically by Soles & Geller 1964 and formally by R&T 1989; no dielectric signature of the inversion at 2.45 GHz |
| H2 A single fixed reference depth as the thickness condition | **EXCLUDED** | No published model uses one; the half-space stress is −EαΔT(z)/(1−ν) pointwise with no reference depth; the construction produces the 879 → 1001 K climb that every onset dataset contradicts; under Beer–Lambert deposition deeper than d it under-predicts compression and can invert sign |
| H3 Two separate inequalities: onset temperature plus a buckling-type thickness condition | **SUPPORTED** | Preston/Dey–Kranz/R&T Eqs. 15–16: flaw span ≥ ~10 × heated-layer thickness with δ ≈ kΔT_s/q; predicts a flat onset above q_min and a sharp cut-off below; the only derived minimum in the literature; Kant 2016's measured minimum (0.45–0.61 MW/m², ~500 °C) matches centimetre-scale flaw spans. Caveat: Dey & Kranz's full text and numerical bound were not obtainable |
| H4 Temperature-dependent absorption causing a solid-state thermal runaway that sharpens deposition to the surface before melting | **EXCLUDED** (for granite) | Measured tan δ rises only ×2 at 500 °C and ×5.6 at 700 °C at 2.45 GHz with d_p still 0.2–0.4 m; conduction-loss scaling makes the fractional rise ≈ 4× weaker at 10 GHz and ≈ 56× weaker at 137 GHz; Woskov's granite stayed partly transparent until melt; runaway is documented only for basalt-type rocks within ~200 K of melting. Caveat: no hot-solid permittivity exists above 3 GHz |
| H5 The interior temperature peak puts the surface skin in tension | **EXCLUDED** (half-space) | Local law gives reduced biaxial compression at a cooler-than-peak surface; steady planar-stress theorem gives σ_rr + σ_θθ = −EαΔT < 0 at the surface; "cold skin in tension" is the finite-body mean-temperature term (+2/3 Eα(T_c − T_s)/(1−ν) for a slab) or grain-scale mismatch or active cooling, none of which is the Beer–Lambert floor |
| H6 Melt at the surface becomes more absorptive rather than reflective | **SUPPORTED** (at mm-wave frequencies) | 28 GHz transmission cut off at melt onset; glassed-surface heating rate 110 vs 7.4 °C/s; melt emissivity (absorbed fraction) 0.66–0.70, 0.91 with trapping; Woskov's model steps absorption up two orders of magnitude. Nuance: surface reflection also rises from 0.14 to 0.3, so "more absorptive" means skin-depth collapse with ~70 % net absorption, not zero reflection; laser glaze behaves oppositely |

H1 is excluded on measurement, not interpretation: the hypothesis predicts that the quartz-richest rock should spall at or above 573 °C and that onset should migrate toward 573 °C with quartz content, and both predictions fail by ~200 °C in the wrong direction ([W&T 1993](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01019868); [R&T 1989](https://doi.org/10.1016/0148-9062(89)90935-2)). H2 is excluded because its only observable consequence, a climbing onset, is absent from Rauenzahn & Tester Fig. 9, Wilkinson & Tester Fig. 15, Kant & von Rohr Fig. 11, Kant et al. 2017 and Höser Fig. 4.11, while its theoretical premise, a stress referenced to a fixed depth, has no counterpart in the half-space solution ([Lu & Fleck 1998](https://www.fleckmech.org/papers/96.pdf)). H3 is supported by the only published derivation of a minimum flux and by the only measured one; what it lacks is the numerical form of the Dey & Kranz bound and a Grimsel flaw-span distribution. H4 is excluded for granite on the Hartlieb/MPN measurement and the frequency scaling argument, with the explicit caveat that the argument above 3 GHz is inference because no hot millimetre-wave permittivity exists; the sharp absorption step is real but belongs to the melt. H5 is excluded for the half-space geometry and would become undecided only if the floor were treated as a finite body on the scale of the spot or actively cooled below ambient. H6 is supported directly by the MIT calorimetry and transmission record.

## Data gaps that no literature closes and that the ~10 GHz experiments can

The absorption coefficient at the operating frequency, hot. No measured complex permittivity of granite exists above 3 GHz at any elevated temperature, and none at 90–140 GHz even cold; the 10 GHz column of the table above is an inference from one room-temperature Barre granite number at 28 GHz scaled by a 2.45 GHz temperature ratio that is probably too steep. The measurement needed is ε′ and ε″ of the user's granite at ~10 GHz (and, if a source is available, in W-band) from room temperature through 1000 °C on heating and on cooling, by cavity perturbation or free-space transmission on a slab, which would settle whether the loss rise is conductive (1/f), fix the penetration depth that governs the deposition profile, test whether the 300 m⁻¹ assumption has any analogue at 10 GHz, and close H4 above 3 GHz. A Woskov-style transmission trace through a slab *during* beam heating would show whether absorption steps at the solidus or rises earlier.

The onset temperature versus intensity curve under volumetric heating. All flat-onset data come from flame and laser heating of a conductively heated skin; no one has measured the first-spall surface temperature under a microwave or millimetre-wave beam on any rock. The experiment is a pyrometer or IR camera at ≥ 100 Hz on the beam spot across at least a decade of intensity bracketing the spall threshold, with the exposure time to onset and the exposure time at which "no spall" is declared both recorded, and the body size stated. If the band is 430–630 °C and flat, the onset is a material property independent of how heat enters, and H2 is excluded a second way; if it climbs, the constraint-depth picture gains the support it currently lacks.

Flake thickness and diameter as a function of intensity on one rock with one method. No experiment anywhere reports flake thickness versus flux on a single rock, no Grimsel thickness exists, and no beam-heated granite flake has been sectioned. Collecting spalls at each intensity, measuring thickness and width by shadow or profilometry as Walsh did, and comparing against the heated-layer depth kΔT_s/q and the inferred absorption depth would test whether volumetric heating thickens the flake toward the absorption depth as Q10 predicts, whether an aspect-ratio floor near the grain size appears, and whether the 1.5 mm cap has any basis.

The spall-to-melt boundary and the melt nucleation depth. The literature gives no spall-to-melt transition for granite under any beam with verified provenance, and the sub-surface melt nucleation 2–4 mm down is reported only at 28 GHz. Sectioning samples stopped just past the transition, together with the intensity and dwell at which the first glass appears, would place the first point on the regime map for a volumetrically heated granite and show whether the melt indeed starts below the surface at 10 GHz. Recording reflected power before and after melt onset would give the 10 GHz analogue of the 0.14 → 0.3 reflection step and the 0.66–0.70 melt absorbed fraction.

Spot size and the radial-crack signature. Q10 predicts a transient hoop-tension ring outside a sharply bounded spot that produces radial cracks and removes nothing, and a reduction of axis compression to ≈ 0.37 S as the thermal penetration approaches the spot radius. Varying spot size at fixed intensity and inspecting for radial cracks at the spot edge versus a flake mosaic inside it would check the mode map and whether δ/a is the right interpolating variable.

Finally, two gaps are laboratory measurements rather than beam experiments but are the model's largest unsupported inputs: a dilatometric α(θ) for Grimsel granite to 800 °C under a modest confining stress, which no source provides and which fixes both the firing temperature and the shape above 570 °C, and hot-tested E and K_Ic above 500 °C on the same rock, which no reachable dataset supplies for any granite. Each is cheaper than the beam campaign and each removes a ±30 % uncertainty that currently sits under the onset prediction.
