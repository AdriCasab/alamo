Simulating How Microwaves Drill Rock

A physics-based computer model of millimetre-wave thermal-spallation drilling, built in the ALAMO framework and tested against laboratory experiments

Project report · MMWSpalling integrator · Prepared September 2026

This report explains, for a general technical reader, what our simulation does, why it is worth doing, how it is built, how we test it, what it has reproduced so far, and what remains open. Wherever an equation appears it is stated plainly and then explained in words.

Abstract We are building a single computer model that simulates how a concentrated beam of energy — a millimetre-wave beam or a flame jet — drills into rock by heating it until thin flakes crack off ("thermal spallation"). Existing models each capture only one piece of this: when the rock first cracks, or an average drilling rate, or the flake sizes. Ours couples heat flow, thermal stress, brittle fracture, and a continuously receding surface in one three-dimensional simulation that is cheap enough to run a full experiment. It is built in the open-source ALAMO framework and validated as a ladder of about twenty steps against four published experiments — Zhang/Oglesby (heating), Hu (prescribed-temperature stress), Kant (failure onset and confining pressure), and Rossi (flake depth) — then applied to the demanding Meier drilling test. The model reproduces heating histories, failure onset and its pressure dependence, and forms a realistic cylindrical hole. Its central open problem — it currently drills roughly 1.9× faster than the real Meier test — is being attacked by the physics we are adding now: debris from detached flakes shading the incoming beam.

1. Why drill rock with microwaves

Deep geothermal energy — tapping the heat stored in hot rock kilometres below the surface — could supply clean, always-on power almost anywhere on Earth. The obstacle is cost. Conventional drilling grinds rock away with a rotating steel bit that physically touches the rock. Deep down, where the rock is hot, hard, and abrasive, those bits wear out quickly. A crew may spend more time pulling kilometres of pipe out of the hole to swap a worn bit than actually drilling. The deeper and hotter the target, the worse this gets, and geothermal's most attractive resources are exactly the deep, hot ones.

Contactless drilling proposes to remove the rock without touching it. Instead of a mechanical bit, a concentrated beam of energy heats the rock surface so fast and so unevenly that the rock breaks itself apart. The two leading energy sources are a high-power flame jet and a millimetre-wave (MMW) beam — essentially a very high-frequency, very high-power microwave, produced by a device called a gyrotron. Because nothing mechanical touches the rock, there is no bit to wear out; the energy source stays safely at the surface and the beam is piped down the hole.

The mechanism that actually removes the rock is called thermal spallation. When you heat one face of a brittle rock very quickly, that thin surface layer tries to expand, but the cold rock beneath holds it back. The trapped expansion builds up enormous compressive stress parallel to the surface. Tiny pre-existing cracks in the rock — every real rock is full of them — concentrate that stress until a thin flake, typically a fraction of a millimetre thick, pops off explosively. Fresh rock is exposed, the beam heats it, and the process repeats. The rock is removed as a shower of small hot flakes ("spall"), not as ground-up powder.

The prize is large but the physics is genuinely hard to predict: it couples heat flow, the mechanical stress that heat creates, the fracture of a flaw-riddled brittle material, and the continuous reshaping of the surface as flakes leave. Our project is a computer model that captures all of these together, in one simulation, and checks its answers against real laboratory experiments.

2. The gap this work fills

There is a substantial published literature on thermal-spallation drilling, but each existing model tends to capture only a slice of the problem. Reviewing the field (and the specific experiments we validate against) shows a consistent pattern:

  • Onset-only models (e.g. Kant 2017, Hu 2018) predict the temperature at which the first flake will pop off, and where. They stop there — they do not simulate the hole actually getting deeper.
  • Analytical rate models (Rauenzahn–Tester, Lyu) estimate an average drilling rate from energy balance, but cannot predict the shape of the hole or the size of the flakes.
  • Grain-scale fracture models (Walsh's GEODYN, Vogler's MOOSE work) resolve individual mineral grains and reproduce the flake-size distribution beautifully, but are so computationally expensive they can only simulate a fraction of a second — not the 23 minutes of a real drilling test.
  • Continuous-damage models (Liu 2024) track a smeared "damage" field but leave the rule for when material actually detaches somewhat arbitrary.

The gap, stated plainly A complete validation of a real drilling experiment needs three things at once: (i) the thermal-mechanical stress physics, (ii) a fracture criterion that decides when rock fails, and (iii) actual material removal with a moving surface. As the Meier (2017) validation packet puts it: "The full set of targets is not hittable by any single model in the literature today — that is the gap this validation case maps out."

Our project targets exactly that gap. We build a single, unified, three-dimensional model that carries the heat, the stress, the fracture criterion, and the receding surface together, cheaply enough to run a full experiment — and we hold it to the same numbers that real experiments report. A second, more subtle contribution is methodological: because the model is assembled from many coupled physical ingredients, we validate it as a ladder of increasingly complete tests, each one isolating one new physical effect against a published experiment before the next is added. That discipline — never adding a physics ingredient without an experimental anchor for it — is itself a contribution to how these multiphysics models should be built and trusted.

3. The physics inside the model

The model solves several physical processes on the same three-dimensional grid, step by step in time. Here are the ingredients, each with its governing idea.

3.1 Heat flow with melting and phase change

Rather than tracking temperature directly, the model tracks enthalpy (total heat content, H), which correctly handles the energy absorbed when rock melts. The heat balance is:

heat∂H/∂t = ∇·(k ∇T) + Q

In words: the heat content of each little cell of rock changes because heat conducts in from neighbours (the ∇·(k∇T) term, where k is thermal conductivity) and because the beam deposits energy there (the source Q). Temperature T is recovered from H through a look-up table that knows the rock's heat capacity and its latent heat of melting.

3.2 The energy beam

The beam's intensity is strongest on the axis and falls off toward the edges, a Gaussian profile. At the surface, the local incident power per unit area at radius r from the beam centre is:

beam profileP(r) = (2P₀ / πw₀²) · exp(−2r² / w₀²)

Here P₀ is the total beam power and w₀ is the beam's radius. Once the energy enters the rock it is absorbed with depth, weaker the deeper it goes, following the Beer–Lambert law:

absorptionQ(z) ∝ α · exp(−αz)

where z is depth and α is the absorption coefficient. We deposit this energy cell by cell using an exact integral rather than sampling at a single point — a fix that mattered, as explained in the results.

3.3 Thermal stress

When the thin surface layer is heated and prevented from expanding by the cold rock below, it develops a large compressive stress parallel to the surface. In the idealised case of a fully confined surface layer, that stress is:

thermal stressσ = −E · αT · (T − Tref) / (1 − ν)

E is the rock's stiffness (Young's modulus), αT its thermal expansion coefficient, ν its Poisson ratio, and (T − Tref) the temperature rise above the cold starting state. The hotter the surface, the larger the stress that wants to break it. The model can compute this either from a full three-dimensional elastic solve or, in confined geometries, from this closed-form relation directly.

3.4 When does a flake pop off? The fracture criterion

This is the heart of the model. Real rock contains microscopic cracks. Fracture mechanics tells us a crack will run when the "stress intensity" at its tip, KI, reaches the rock's fracture toughness, KIc. We define a dimensionless spallation number:

criterionSp = KI / KIc(T)   →   a flake detaches when Sp ≥ 1

The driving side KI is computed by integrating the compressive stress over the depth of a pre-existing crack of length a. The resisting side KIc(T) is the rock's toughness, which we take from published measurements and which drops sharply as the rock gets hotter (from about 1.4 MPa·m½ at room temperature down to about 0.2 near 850 °C for granite). So heat attacks the rock twice: it raises the driving stress and it weakens the resistance.

For the special case of a uniformly heated, fully confined surface, this criterion collapses to a clean closed form for the temperature rise at which the first flake pops (the Kant relation):

onsetΔTonset = KIc(1 − ν) / (2 · 1.763 · √(a/π) · E · αT)

We use this exact relation as a machine-precision check on the full numerical model — if the code and the algebra disagree, something is wrong in the code.

3.5 Two ways to describe failure

The model actually carries two independent descriptions of rock failure, selectable at runtime, because the literature uses both and each anchors to different experiments:

  • Continuous damage (the "damage-law" path). Built on the Drucker–Prager criterion — a standard way to describe how stiff, brittle materials yield under combined pressure and shear. Instead of a sharp on/off crack, each cell accumulates a continuous damage value from 0 (intact) to 1 (fully failed), which in turn softens the rock's stiffness. This path is anchored to the Hu prescribed-temperature experiments (§6.2).
  • Fracture-mechanics spallation (the "Weibull" path). The crisp Sp = KI/KIc criterion of §3.4 combined with the statistical flaw distribution described next (§3.6), deciding discretely when an individual flake detaches. This path is anchored to the Kant onset and Rossi flake-depth experiments (§6.3–6.4).

Having both descriptions share the same heat and stress machinery lets us validate against the full spread of published experiments and cross-check one against the other. A strict rule keeps them honest: turning on a new capability must leave every test on the other path bit-for-bit unchanged.

3.6 Real rock is not uniform: flaws and microstructure

Two rocks of the same type do not fail at exactly the same temperature, because their internal cracks differ. We capture this with two ingredients. First, a Voronoi microstructure — the grid is partitioned into randomly shaped mineral grains, each with its own properties and grain boundaries, mimicking a real rock's texture. Second, every surface cell is assigned a random starting crack length drawn from a Weibull distribution (the standard statistical model for the strength of brittle materials). A subtle but important consequence:

Extreme-value statistics govern first failure With many surface cells, the one that fails first holds not the average crack, but the largest crack present — approximately amax ≈ a₀ · (ln N)1/m for N cells. First failure therefore happens at a lower temperature than a single-crack calculation predicts. Getting this right required scoring the model against the realised largest flaw, not the nominal average — a correction that reshaped one of our validation steps.

3.7 Removing material and moving the surface

When the criterion fires in a column of the grid, the model computes how deep the detaching flake is, marks that rock as removed, and lets the surface recede. The next time step, the beam and heat solve see the new, deeper surface. This "moving boundary" is what lets a single run simulate a hole getting steadily deeper — the ingredient most published models lack.

3.8 The newest ingredient: melt-aware spallation

You cannot spall a liquid — a melt has no rigid skeleton to store the crack-driving stress. Until recently the criterion ignored this and would happily "spall" molten rock. We added a load-bearing skeleton factor that scales the driving stress down as the rock melts:

skeletonf(ΛL) = max(0,  1 − ΛL / Λcrit)

ΛL is the local liquid fraction and Λcrit ≈ 0.4 is the point at which a partially molten rock loses rigidity. In fully solid rock f = 1 and nothing changes; as the rock melts f → 0 and the crack-driving stress vanishes, so the criterion correctly refuses to spall liquid.

3.9 Two removal regimes and the drilling rate

At moderate heating the rock spalls — solid flakes crack off. At very high heating the surface can instead vaporise, boiling away as gas. The model handles both, and at each surface cell decides which regime is active, so a single headline number — the rate of penetration (how fast the hole deepens, in metres per hour) — emerges from whichever mechanism dominates locally. The rate of penetration is exactly what a real drilling test reports, and reproducing it is the goal of the Meier case (§7).

4. How the software is built

The model is an integrator (a self-contained physics driver) inside ALAMO, an open-source scientific computing framework built on the U.S. Department of Energy's AMReX library. Two features of the framework do heavy lifting:

  • Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR). Interesting physics happens in a thin, moving layer at the drilling surface; the rock metres away barely changes. AMR automatically places a fine computational grid where it is needed (near the hot, cracking surface) and a coarse grid elsewhere, so we spend computer time where it matters.
  • Parallel computing. The domain is split across many processors that run simultaneously and exchange information at their shared boundaries. A recurring theme in our development has been ensuring answers are identical no matter how the domain is split — a strict correctness test we return to constantly.

The physics ingredients from Section 3 are layered onto a single simulation class using a design pattern (virtual multiple inheritance) that lets heat conduction, mechanics, and removal coexist. One hard-won rule governs all of it:

A rule written in scar tissue Early attempts to "simplify" the shared base classes caused subtle crashes deep inside the mesh-refinement machinery. The standing rule is now: never restructure the inheritance graph. New physics is layered on top; the foundation is not touched. When the main file grew unwieldy (~4,400 lines), we split pieces out into included sub-files without altering that foundation, proving each split changed nothing by comparing outputs byte-for-byte.

4.1 A disciplined three-phase workflow

Every increment of work follows a strict three-role loop, each role a fresh, focused session:

  1. Plan — write a precise specification of the next step: the exact physics, equations, file locations, and the experimental numbers it must reproduce.
  2. Implement — write the code and tests to that specification, then record completion notes and lessons learned.
  3. Verify — an independent review checks the implementation against the specification and returns "accepted" or "rejected."

This separation keeps each step small, specified, and reviewable, and it produces a written trail explaining why every design choice was made — valuable for a model that will be built on for years.

5. The development process

The model was built as a staged ladder of about twenty steps, each adding one physical ingredient and validating it before the next. The guiding principle is that every new default-off feature must leave all previous tests bit-for-bit identical — so new physics can never silently corrupt what already worked. The rough arc:

  1. Heat and beam (Steps 1–4). The enthalpy heat solver, the Gaussian/Beer–Lambert beam, and surface heat losses — validated against textbook solutions and the Zhang/Oglesby microwave-heating experiment.
  2. Microstructure and stress (Steps 5–7). Voronoi mineral grains, heat conduction through a heterogeneous rock, and the thermal-stress solve — validated against Hu's prescribed-temperature experiments.
  3. Damage and fracture (Steps 8–9, 12, 15). Two failure descriptions: a continuous-damage law, and the fracture-mechanics spallation number of Section 3.4, plus grain-boundary cohesion and the Weibull flaw statistics.
  4. Removal and rate (Steps 10–11, 16–19). Detaching flakes, the moving surface, vaporisation, and the combined rate of penetration — validated against the Rossi flake-depth and Kant onset experiments, and extended to run under a real microwave beam.
  5. Confinement and geometry (Steps 18, 20). The effect of underground pressure on failure, and a surface-normal removal kernel that lets a realistic cylindrical hole emerge on its own.

Interspersed are honest "we hit a wall" records. For example, running the mechanics solve on a mesh refined only in the vertical direction never converged; the fix was to keep mechanically-active meshes cubic and use a physically-motivated confined geometry instead. These dead ends are documented as carefully as the successes, so they are not repeated.

6.1 Heating: the Zhang/Oglesby microwave experiment

The first real test is whether the heat and beam physics reproduce a measured surface-temperature history when granite is heated by a microwave beam whose power is cycled up and down. The model tracks both the independent published simulation and the physical experiment across the entire power-cycled run, including the sharp cooldown when the beam switches off.

6.2 Prescribed-temperature stress and breakage: the Hu experiment

Before trusting the model to decide when rock fails, we check that it computes the right stresses and the right first-failure timing under a controlled surface heating. Hu's experiments heat granite and sandstone along a known surface-temperature history and report where and when breakage begins. The model reproduces the subsurface temperature rise, the surface-stress profile, and — importantly — the correct ordering: granite (stronger and stiffer) reaches breakage onset later than sandstone, with both inside the experimentally reported windows.

6.3 Onset and confinement: the Kant experiment

Next we validate, on the fracture-mechanics path, at what temperature the first flake pops. For Central Aare granite the model predicts a first-failure temperature rise of about 461 °C, inside the experimentally reported 390–560 °C band, and matches the closed-form onset relation (§3.4) to within about 3%. The random flaw distribution shows up visibly: failure begins as a scattered, patchy pattern at the weakest cells rather than as a uniform front.

Underground, rock is squeezed from all sides. Applying a confining pressure raises the temperature the surface must reach before it can spall, because the confinement partly offsets the crack-driving thermal stress. The model reproduces this trend quantitatively across a pressure sweep, with the onset-versus-pressure slope within about 2% of the analytical prediction.

6.4 Flake depth: the Rossi experiment

The Rossi experiment measures how deep the detaching flakes are. This test surfaced an honest and instructive limitation rather than a clean pass. The model correctly produces a large population of first-failure events dominated by grain-boundary cracking, but at our working grid resolution (cells ~1.56 mm tall) the predicted flake depths cluster around 775 µm, whereas the experiment peaks at 100–200 µm. Three independent attempts to reconstruct the sub-cell stress all converged on the same conclusion: the flake depth we are trying to resolve is finer than a single grid cell, so no clever sub-cell trick can manufacture it — only a genuinely finer vertical mesh can, and that reopens the solver-convergence problem noted above.

6.5 The full chain under a real microwave beam

Bringing the ingredients together, the model drills a full three-dimensional wellbore under a microwave beam through a Voronoi granite microstructure — heat, stress, fracture, and a receding surface all at once. A hot spot forms under the beam, thermal stress builds, grain-scale cracking removes flakes, and a wellbore opens and deepens through the mineral grains.

6.6 An emergent cylindrical hole

A recent addition — a surface-normal removal kernel that lets each patch of surface recede along its own local normal direction — produces a qualitatively correct result the earlier column-by-column removal could not: a roughly cylindrical hole emerges on its own, rather than a funnel-shaped crater, and the spallation number rides steadily at ~1 along the drilling face without stalling or overheating. This matters because a cylindrical hole is exactly what the Meier experiment reports, and shape is one of the harder targets to reproduce.

A quantitative fix that mattered While preparing the Meier case we found the beam deposition was sampling absorption at a single point per cell, which lost a large fraction of the beam's power in cells where absorption was strong (roughly 46% loss in the worst cases; runs were receiving only ~54% of nominal power). Replacing it with an exact per-cell integral of the Beer–Lambert law restored energy conservation — and, importantly, invalidated earlier calibrations that had unknowingly compensated for the missing power. This is a good example of the validation ladder catching a bug that a single end-to-end test would have hidden inside a fudge factor.

7. The current frontier: the Meier drilling experiment

The most demanding validation is Meier's 2017 pilot demonstration: a real flame-jet drilling test on a 660 kg block of Grimsel granite. Over 23 minutes and 38 kW of input power, the hole advanced 50 cm through the block at a steady 1.5 metres per hour, producing a cylindrical hole about 85 mm across and removing 3.4 litres of rock. It reports not just whether the rock spalls but the sustained drilling rate, the energy efficiency, the hole shape, and the flake sizes — the complete set of targets no single prior model could hit.

Our model reproduces the qualitative behaviour (steady drilling, an emergent cylinder, no runaway) and gets the drilling rate to the right order of magnitude. But there is a clear, honest discrepancy:

The central open problem With no bit and clean removal, the model drills roughly 1.9× too fast — a predicted ~2.7 m/h against the measured 1.5 m/h. Equivalently, to match the real rate the beam would have to deliver energy at only ~17% efficiency. The model removes rock too efficiently per joule delivered. The prime suspect is a piece of missing physics: in reality, detached flakes pile up on the surface and block the incoming beam until they are cleared away — Meier literally had to add a vacuum cleaner mid-test to remove them. Our model makes every flake vanish instantly and cleanly, so 100% of the beam always reaches fresh rock.

This diagnosis — that cuttings shielding is the leading candidate for the over-prediction — is what sets the current work agenda.

8. What we are working on now

We have organised the remaining physics into a prioritised program (labelled P1–P8) attached to the Meier case. The two most recent items:

8.1 P1 — melt-aware spallation (completed)

This is the ingredient described in Section 3.8: the load-bearing skeleton factor that stops the criterion from spalling liquid rock. It is implemented, tested, and review-accepted, and it produced a genuinely surprising physical finding:

Key finding: a self-regulating "thermostat" When removal is active, the drilling surface regulates its own temperature. Every flake that pops off exposes cooler rock underneath, so the surface never actually climbs to the melting point — at any beam power, spallation wins the race and keeps the surface pinned near its ~850 K spall-onset temperature. Melt therefore never appears in normal drilling; it only shows up where removal is artificially blocked. This tells us that the melting regime is not reachable in this model until a different feedback (temperature-dependent absorption, item P3) is added — which reorders the whole to-do list below.

8.2 P4 — cuttings/debris shielding (active, the prime suspect)

This is the step that directly attacks the 1.9× over-prediction. The idea is to give each column of the surface a debris load Σ (mass of flakes per unit area sitting on it). Each time step it grows when a flake is added and decays as debris is swept away with a characteristic clearance time τ:

debrisΣn+1 = Σn · exp(−Δt/τ) + ρ · hspall

That debris then shades the beam using the same Beer–Lambert idea as light through a dusty layer:

shieldingη = exp(−Σ / Σ₀),    P → P · η

where Σ₀ sets how much debris is needed to dim the beam appreciably. This creates exactly the negative feedback the experiment implies: drill faster → make more debris → block more beam → drill slower, settling at a self-limited rate. The step is default-off (so every existing test stays identical) and the acceptance target is simple: with a physically plausible Σ₀, the predicted rate should move from the no-shield baseline down toward the measured 1.5 m/h. The specification is written; implementation is the immediate next task.

Two housekeeping items are also pending: the completed P1 and a behaviour-preserving code refactor are implemented and tested but not yet committed to version control, so the immediate practical next actions are to commit that work and then implement P4.

10. Honest limitations

A report that only listed successes would be misleading. The model's current, openly-tracked limitations are:

  • Rate over-prediction (~1.9×) against Meier — the central open problem, being attacked by the active P4 debris-shielding step.
  • Grid-resolution sensitivity. The sustained drilling rate still varies by ±30–40% depending on cell size and is not yet fully converged; production numbers carry roughly a factor-1.4 numerical uncertainty. Understanding and closing this is a standing task.
  • Flake depth (Rossi). Predicted flakes are thicker than measured, a mesh-resolution limit that three independent reconstructions confirmed cannot be closed at the current vertical resolution.
  • The melt regime is not yet a validated capability — it is unreachable in normal drilling until temperature-dependent absorption is added, and we currently lack the granite absorption data to add it responsibly.
  • The fast stress model has known blind spots. The efficient closed-form stress cannot see stress concentrations, block-scale splitting, or grain-scale heterogeneity; a planned A/B against a full elastic solve will quantify the error being carried.

Each of these is written down, understood, and mapped to a specific planned step — which is the point of the ladder-and-record methodology.

11. Summary

This project builds a single, unified, three-dimensional simulation of millimetre-wave thermal-spallation rock drilling — heat flow, thermal stress, fracture of a flaw-riddled brittle rock, and a continuously receding surface, all coupled and cheap enough to run a real experiment end-to-end. That combination is precisely what the existing literature lacks: prior models capture onset, or rate, or flake size, or grain-scale fracture, but not all of it together with a moving boundary.

The model has climbed a roughly twenty-step validation ladder, reproducing microwave-heating temperature histories (Zhang/Oglesby), spallation onset and its pressure dependence (Kant), grain-boundary-dominated fracture (Rossi), and full three-dimensional wellbore formation under a real beam. Its limitations — a rate over-prediction against the demanding Meier drilling test, grid-resolution sensitivity, and mesh-limited flake depth — are documented rather than hidden, and each maps to a specific planned step.

The active frontier is the Meier case. A recently completed step (melt-aware spallation) revealed that the drilling surface self-regulates its own temperature, which reordered the remaining work. The current step (cuttings/debris shielding) adds the physics most likely to explain why the model presently drills ~1.9× too fast: real flakes blanket the surface and block the beam until cleared. If that negative feedback pulls the predicted rate toward the measured 1.5 m/h with a physically plausible parameter, the model will, for the first time in this literature, reproduce the full set of a real drilling experiment's targets in one simulation.
